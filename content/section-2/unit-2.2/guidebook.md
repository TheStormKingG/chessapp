# Unit 2.2 — Forks

## The habit

**Level two begins here — basic tactics are now allowed.**

Unit 2.1 was about not losing pieces. This unit is the other half: taking them.
From here on the bot you play is allowed to use tactics of its own, so the
threat scan you learned last unit is not optional any more. Run it, then look
for a fork.

## What a fork is

A fork is one piece attacking two at once.

That is the whole idea. Your opponent gets one move. If you attack two things
that both matter, he saves one and you take the other. It is the cheapest way
to win material in chess, and it is the tactic you will meet most often —
both as the one playing it and as the one walking into it.

Two things make a fork work, and both have to be true:

1. **Both targets are worth taking.** Forking a pawn and a pawn wins you a pawn
   at best, and usually nothing.
2. **The forking square is safe.** If the square is defended, you have not
   forked anything — you have given a piece away.

Beginners get the first one right and the second one wrong. Before you play a
fork, ask the question from unit 2.1: *after I land there, what does he have?*

## The knight fork

The knight is the best forker on the board, for one reason: nothing can block
it, and neither of the two pieces it hits can take it back unless it happens to
be another knight or a king standing right next door.

A queen forking a rook and a bishop can often be answered — the bishop moves
and defends the rook on the way. A knight forking them cannot.

So hunt for the **square**, not for the capture. Look at the enemy pieces you
would like to win, and ask which single square attacks two of them. A knight on
f7 attacks d8 and h8. A knight on e7 attacks c8 and g8. Those two patterns
alone will win you games.

The best forks are the ones that come with **check**, because a check cannot be
ignored. The second piece has no time to run.

## The pawn fork

A pawn attacks the two squares diagonally in front of it. Push it so that both
of those squares are occupied and you have forked with the cheapest piece you
own. Nobody wants to trade a knight for a pawn, so the pawn nearly always wins
the exchange.

One rule makes the difference between a pawn fork and a blunder: **push a
defended pawn.** An undefended pawn that lands next to the enemy king is simply
taken. A pawn defended by another pawn cannot be taken by the king at all.

Pawns cannot fork a bishop or a queen easily, and the reason is worth knowing:
a bishop or a queen standing on one of the forked squares is already attacking
the square your pawn wants to land on.

## The queen fork

The queen moves in eight directions, so she forks more often than anything
else. Any two of her lines will do: two diagonals, a file and a diagonal, a
rank and a file.

The square you want most often is the one where two long diagonals cross. From
d5, a white queen looks at a8 one way and g8 the other — and enemy kings and
rooks spend a lot of the game on exactly those squares.

But she is worth nine, so the rule from above bites hardest here. **A queen
fork from a defended square is not a fork, it is a gift.** Check the square
before you check the king.

## The family fork

When a knight hits the king, the queen and a rook all at the same time, that
is the **family fork** — the whole family at once.

It is the same hunt as any knight fork, only with a bigger prize. The squares
to look at are the ones near an uncastled king: c7 and f7, d6 and e6. A king on
e8 with a rook on a8 and a queen on e6 loses to a knight landing on c7.

## Setting up a fork with a check

Most of the time the fork is not there yet: one of the two squares you can hit
is empty, or the king is standing one square away from where you need him.

A check fixes that, because a check cannot be ignored.

There are two versions, and they are worth learning as a pair:

- **Drive the king onto a target square.** You want your knight on f7, which
  attacks d8 and h8. His queen is on d8 already, but his king is on g8. Check
  him, and if his only square is h8, the fork is on.
- **Force a piece onto a target square.** The king is on h8 already, but d8 is
  empty. Put a rook on d8 with check. If his queen is the only piece that can
  take it, she has to — and now she is standing on d8, where the knight on f7
  hits her.

The second one costs a rook and wins a queen. That is a good trade, and it is
the first time in this course that giving something up on purpose is the right
move.

## Before every move

- What is he threatening? (unit 2.1 — still first)
- Which of his pieces are worth winning?
- Is there one square that attacks two of them?
- Is that square safe?
- If the fork is nearly there, does a check finish it?
