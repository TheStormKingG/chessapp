# Unit 2.4 — Back-rank and helper mates

## The habit

**Make an escape square for your king after you have developed.**

You castle, and the king sits behind three pawns that have never moved. That is
safe against almost everything — and completely lost to a rook arriving on the
back rank, because the king has nowhere to step.

One pawn move fixes it. Push the h-pawn, or the g-pawn, one square. Now a check
along the first rank is a check you can walk away from instead of a checkmate.

Do it once, early, when nothing urgent is happening. Players who wait until the
rook is already coming find they have no time for it.

## The back-rank weakness

Three pawns in front of a castled king cover the squares the king would run to.
So a check along the eighth rank (or the first, if it is your king) has three
answers, and a castled king usually has none of them:

1. **Move the king** — every square is a pawn of your own.
2. **Block the check** — you need a piece that can reach the rank in one move.
3. **Take the checking piece** — you need a piece that attacks it.

When none of the three is available, the check is mate. That is the whole of
the back-rank mate, and it ends more beginner games than any other pattern.

Two things make it worse than it looks. Your own pawns cause it, so it arrives
without warning. And it costs nothing to prevent.

## Helper mates

Every mate in this unit is the same idea: a piece gives check next to the king,
and **something else stops the king taking it.** A queen on her own beside the
king is not mate — the king simply eats her. A queen beside the king with a
bishop behind her is mate.

That second piece is the helper. Learn to look for it, in both directions: when
you want to mate, find the helper first; when you are being attacked, take the
helper and the mate disappears.

### Support mate

The queen steps next to the king and a rook, a bishop, a knight or a pawn
defends her. The king cannot take, cannot move, and nothing can capture the
queen. It is the most common finish in beginner games.

### Corridor mate

The back-rank mate turned sideways. The king is in a one-square-wide corridor —
its own pieces on the file beside it — and a rook checks along that corridor
from the far end. The king can only move up and down the corridor, and the rook
covers every square of it.

### Smothered mate

The king is completely surrounded by its own pieces. No other piece can give
check without being taken, but a knight jumps over the wall, and nothing on the
board can reach the square it lands on.

Only a knight can do this, and that is exactly why it is worth knowing: it is
the one mate your own pieces build for the enemy.

### Damiano's mate

A pawn on g6 defends h7. The queen lands on h7 next to the king, and the pawn
is the helper. The king cannot take, f8 is its own rook, and g7 and h8 are the
queen's.

It is the oldest recorded mate in chess, written down in 1512, and beginners
walk into it every day because the pawn on g6 looks harmless.

## Using this both ways

When you are the one being mated, the questions are the same, backwards:

- Has my king got a square? If not, make one now.
- What is defending the piece that wants to sit next to my king? Take it.
- Which of my own pieces are taking squares away from my king?

A mate you can name is a mate you can see coming.
