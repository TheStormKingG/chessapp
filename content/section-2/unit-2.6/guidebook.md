# Unit 2.6 — Opening principles and your first opening

## The habit

**When an enemy bishop or knight lands on g4, g5, b4 or b5, ask it where it is
going with the flank pawn.**

Those four squares are where a bishop or a knight goes to lean on your position
— pinning a knight, eyeing your king, sitting where you would rather it did not.
And all four of them can be hit by a pawn that has not moved yet: h3 and h6
answer g4 and g5, a3 and a6 answer b4 and b5.

The pawn move costs you almost nothing. The piece has to take, retreat, or be
taken, and while it decides, you have gained a move.

Ask the question early, before the piece has settled in. A bishop on g5 that has
been there for six moves has usually already done its job.

## The three jobs of an opening

There are only three, and you can do them in almost any sensible order.

1. **Put a pawn in the middle.** e4 and d4 for White, e5 and d5 for Black. A
   pawn there takes squares away from the enemy and opens lines for your own
   pieces.
2. **Bring the knights and bishops out.** Off the back rank, onto squares where
   they attack something or hold a middle square. All four of them, not one.
3. **Castle.** The king leaves the middle and a rook comes towards it, in one
   move. Do it by about move ten.

Nothing else in the opening is as important as these three, and most beginner
games are decided by one side doing them and the other side not.

## The two mistakes

### Do not move the same piece twice

Every opening move should bring a **new** piece to a **new** square. Move one
piece twice and you have spent two moves developing one piece, while your
opponent has developed two.

There is one honest exception: if a piece is attacked, or if moving it again
wins material, then move it. "Do not move it twice" is a rule about wasting
moves, not a rule about ignoring threats.

### Do not bring the queen out early

The queen is worth nine points, so every piece worth three can chase her. Bring
her out on move two and your opponent develops a knight to attack her, you move
her, he develops another piece to attack her, you move her again. He has three
pieces out and you have none.

It looks frightening the first few times because an early queen attacks f7 or
f2 — the one square by the king that nothing but the king defends. The answer is
almost never to panic. It is to **develop a piece that defends**, so that you
are building your position while your opponent is moving one piece about.

## Your first opening: the Italian

As White, three moves, playable nearly every game:

1. **e4** — a pawn in the middle.
2. **Nf3** — a knight out, attacking the pawn on e5, and the road to castling is
   half clear.
3. **Bc4** — a bishop out, pointing through d5 and e6 at f7, and now White can
   castle.

Then d3, Nc3, castle, and Be3 or Bg5. You do not need to learn any further than
that.

## As Black: the same pawn back

- Against **1.e4**, play **1...e5**.
- Against **1.d4**, play **1...d5**.

You take your share of the middle, the two pawns face each other and neither can
capture, and you have opened a line for a bishop and for the queen. From there
develop the knights and bishops and castle, exactly as White does.

Against 1.d4 there is one extra thing worth knowing: get the bishop on c8 out
**before** you play a pawn to e6, or you will shut it in behind its own pawns.

## The target position

This is what you are aiming at, and it is worth more than any list of moves:

- pawns holding the middle,
- both knights and both bishops off the back rank,
- the king castled,
- and **nothing standing between your two rooks**.

That last one is the part beginners forget. The rooks only join up when the
knight, the bishop and the **queen** have all left the back rank. A queen sitting
on d1 with everything else developed means the opening is not finished yet.

When you reach that picture, every piece you own has somewhere useful to go, and
you can start looking for a plan. Until you reach it, the plan is to reach it.
