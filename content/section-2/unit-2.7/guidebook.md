# Unit 2.7 — Endgame rules that decide games

## The habit

**Activate the king and attack pawns in the endgame.**

For six units the advice has been to keep the king out of the way. Castle early,
put a pawn in front of it, do not walk it into the open. All of that is correct
while the queens and rooks are on the board.

When they come off, the same king becomes one of the strongest pieces you own —
worth roughly a knight and a half, and able to attack a pawn from any direction.
A game between two players of equal strength is very often decided by which of
them remembered to start walking first.

So when a queen trade leaves you in an ending, make the king's journey your
first thought, not your last.

## What can and cannot mate

Before you plan anything, know what the material on the board is capable of.

| Material | Against a bare king |
| --- | --- |
| King and queen | Mates |
| King and rook | Mates |
| King and two bishops | Mates |
| King, bishop and knight | Mates, but it is hard |
| King and one bishop | **Cannot mate** |
| King and one knight | **Cannot mate** |
| King and two knights | **Cannot be forced** |

The last three are drawn positions, not difficult ones. No amount of skill wins
them, and recognising that saves you from playing on for an hour — and, more
usefully, tells you when a trade down to a lone bishop throws away a win.

Two knights deserve a word. You can build a mating net with them, but on the
move the net closes the defending king has no legal move, and stalemate is a
draw. Only a blunder by the defender turns it into a win.

## The rule of the square

A pawn runs; a king chases. You do not have to count the race move by move.

Count how many moves the pawn still needs, and draw a box that many squares
across, starting at the pawn and running towards the queening square. If the
defending king is inside the box — or can step inside it on its move — the pawn
is caught. If not, push.

The rule works because a king crosses a diagonal in the same time it crosses a
file, so its real distance to the queening square is just the larger of the two
gaps. The box reads that off at a glance.

One warning that costs beginners games: the box is drawn from the pawn's current
square, and it shrinks every time the pawn moves. Check it before each push, not
once at the start.

## King in front of the pawn, and direct opposition

A pawn cannot escort itself. In a king-and-pawn ending the king is the escort,
and the fight is over one question: whose king gets in front of the pawn.

The attacker wants the king **ahead** of its own pawn, ideally on the sixth
rank. Once it is there the pawn promotes, whoever is to move.

The defender wants the king **directly in the pawn's path**, facing the enemy
king with exactly one square between them. In that stand-off the side who must
move is the side who gives ground — the one situation in chess where having the
move is a liability. Take it, and the game is drawn. Lose it, and the enemy king
walks past you.

That is the whole of it: get in front, or stand in front.

## The rook-pawn draw

A pawn on the a-file or the h-file is worth less than any other pawn, and
sometimes worth nothing at all.

If the defending king reaches the corner square the pawn is running to, the game
is drawn — an extra pawn, a perfectly placed king and best play will not change
it. To push a king off a square you need room on both sides of it, and beyond
the edge file there is no room.

Move that same position one file inwards and the attacker wins comfortably,
because now there is a file on the far side to work with. Same kings, same
distance, opposite result.

Two things follow. As the defender, run for the corner **in front of** the pawn —
never the corner behind you, and never away from the pawn. As the attacker, think
hard before trading into an ending where your last pawn is a rook pawn.

## The promotion race

When both sides have a passed pawn, stop defending and start counting.

Count the moves each pawn needs. Equal counts go to the side to move. Then check
the two things counting alone misses:

1. **Is anything standing on the road?** A king sitting on the file in front of a
   pawn stops it completely, and the pawn is not really passed at all.
2. **Does the new queen arrive with check?** A queen that promotes with check
   wins a whole tempo, and that turns a lost race into a won one.

In a genuine race, a single quiet move is fatal. It does not cost you a pawn; it
costs you the tie-break, which is the same thing as the game.

## Activate the king

Everything above is the same idea seen from different angles: in an endgame the
king decides things. So put it to work.

- Trade into an ending and your first move should start the king's walk.
- Head for the centre, then for whichever wing the pawns need you on.
- Count the journey. If your king reaches the target two moves before theirs,
  you are the one attacking and they are the one defending.
- An outside passed pawn plus an active king is usually decisive on its own.

There is nothing to be afraid of. Once the queens and rooks are gone, work out
what is actually left that could check your king — and most of the time the
answer is nothing at all.
