# Unit 2.8 — Notation, the clock and slow games

## The habit

**Manage the clock.**

A slow game hands you minutes and almost every beginner gives them back
unspent. Losing with twenty minutes left is the commonest way a beginner loses
a game they understood.

The habit is two sentences long. The first move you see is a candidate, not an
answer. Before you touch the piece, work out what your opponent gets to play in
reply.

That check costs about ten seconds. It is the cheapest ten seconds in chess,
and it is worth exactly as much when the answer is "yes, it is safe" as when
the answer is "no, it loses a rook".

## Reading and writing a move

A written move names **the piece and the square it lands on** — never the
square it came from.

| Written | Means |
|---|---|
| `Nf3` | A knight goes to f3 |
| `e4` | A pawn goes to e4 (pawns have no letter) |
| `Bxc6` | The bishop takes whatever is on c6 |
| `exd5` | The pawn from the e-file takes on d5 |
| `O-O` | Castling kingside: the king ends on g1 (or g8) |
| `O-O-O` | Castling queenside: the king ends on c1 (or c8) |
| `Qh5+` | The queen goes to h5 and gives check |
| `Rxd8#` | The rook takes on d8 and it is mate |

Two things are worth saying out loud, because they are the two that confuse
people.

**A pawn capture borrows the file it left.** `exd5` starts with a small `e`
because a pawn has no letter of its own, and without the file there would be
nothing to tell one pawn from another. That is the only ordinary case where the
starting square appears at all.

**When two identical pieces can reach the same square, the file is added.**
Both rooks can get to d1, so the move is `Rad1` or `Rfd1`. If they share a
file, the rank is used instead: `R1d2`.

Piece letters are K, Q, R, B and N. The knight takes N because the king already
has K.

## Using most of your time

The clock is not a score. Time left on it at the end of the game is worth
nothing at all, so the only question is whether you spent it on the right
moves.

Three things go wrong at this level, and they are all the same thing:

1. **A capture that looks free.** You see `Rxe5` in one second. Spend the next
   ten looking at every black piece that can reach e5. A pawn is the defender
   people miss, because the eye goes to the big pieces first.
2. **A threat that makes you run.** Your rook is attacked, so you move it — and
   the queen that attacked it was standing undefended on the same file. A
   hurried move misses good things as often as it walks into bad ones.
3. **Fear near your own king.** Your opponent threatens mate next move, so you
   panic. It is still your turn. Count who gets there first before you decide
   you are lost.

The counter-habit is small and it is mechanical: **name the reply before you
move.** Not "is this a good move" — that question is too big to answer in ten
seconds. Just "what does he play now".

Keep enough time back not to flag. Beyond that, spend it.

## Going over your own game

The app does the analysis for you. Finish a game and open the review from the
play screen: it marks every move, gives an accuracy for each side and the point
at which the game left the opening book, and hands back up to five key moments
with the position as it stood **before** your move, so you can try again before
it shows you the answer. Every mistake and blunder goes into your error log,
and where there were enough of them the review ends with a short drill built
out of your own errors.

None of that is work you have to do yourself. **Reading it in the right order
is**, and there is only one rule:

> Go to the **earliest** mistake, not the biggest one.

A blunder on move thirty usually happens because the position was already lost
on move twelve. The move-thirty lesson is one you will never meet again; the
move-twelve lesson is one you repeat every game.

Two labels confuse people:

- **Book** is not praise or blame. It means the move was still in the opening
  book, so it was not your decision yet. Your own game starts where the book
  ends, and the summary tells you where that was.
- **Best** means best *in the position it was played in*. A blunder followed by
  three Best moves is completely normal — it is the blunder you go back to.

Then do the one thing the review cannot do for you: **say out loud what you
missed.** Not "I blundered a rook", which teaches nothing. "I left a piece on a
diagonal the bishop was already looking down." A mistake you can name is a
mistake you can see coming.

## Putting the three together

They are one loop, and the unit is in this order on purpose.

Notation lets you write the game down, so there is something to go back to.
The clock is what buys you the moves worth writing down. The review tells you
which of them to look at first — and what it hands back, nearly every time, is
a position where ten seconds would have been enough.
