# Unit 2.1 — Real Chess

## The habit

**Look at the opponent's threats before your own idea.**

Everything in Section 1 was about your side of the board: how your pieces move,
what they attack, how to take a free piece. That is half a game. The other half
is sitting opposite you, and it moves too.

This unit turns the board around. Every question in it is about what *they*
have.

## Their last move

A move is never only theirs. When a piece lands on a new square it attacks
squares it did not attack a moment ago, and one of those squares may have
something of yours on it.

So the first question after their move is not "what is my plan" but **"what
does that piece hit now?"** Follow the lines it travels on. Stop at the first
piece of yours you meet.

Most of the time the answer is nothing, and you carry on. The move only has to
matter once.

## The threat scan

Before you choose a move, run a list for them, in this order:

1. **Checks.** What checks do they have? A check cannot be ignored, so it comes
   first.
2. **Captures.** What can they take? List them all, even the bad ones.
3. **Threats.** What are they setting up for the move after?

Listing a capture is not the same as fearing it. A capture onto a square you
defend twice is on the list and then crossed off. The point of the list is that
nothing arrives as a surprise.

## Hanging on both sides

A piece is **hanging** when it is attacked and nothing defends it. In unit 1.2
you learned to look for theirs. Now look for yours in the same breath.

Positions usually have loose pieces on both sides at once. A board with three
of Black's hanging and two of White's is normal, not unusual. Count theirs,
then count yours — and do not stop after the first one you find.

## Counting on a contested square

When both sides pile onto one square, count:

1. How many of your pieces **attack** it.
2. How many of their pieces **defend** it.
3. More attackers than defenders means you can win the square.
4. Equal numbers means leave it alone — everything comes off in pairs and
   nobody gains.

Take with your **cheapest** piece first. Whatever you put on the square is what
they capture, so send the pawn, not the rook.

## The sanity check

You have found a move you like. One question is left, and it is the one people
skip:

> **After I play this, what do they have?**

Run their checks and their captures against the board as it will be, not as it
is. Two things catch people out:

- A capture that wins a piece and loses the game. The piece you moved was
  doing a job — guarding a back rank, blocking a file — and moving it gave the
  job up.
- A check that looks free. A check is still a move, and the square it lands on
  needs the same question as any other square.

## Before every move

- What did their last move attack?
- What checks and captures do they have?
- What is hanging — theirs, and mine?
- After my move, what do they have?

Four questions. They take a few seconds, and they are the difference between
playing chess and playing solitaire on a board with someone else on it.
