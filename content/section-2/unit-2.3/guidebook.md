# Unit 2.3 — Pins and skewers

## The habit

**Keep a pin.**

A pinned piece is not going anywhere. That is the whole point of it, and it is
also the reason beginners throw the advantage away: they see a piece that
cannot move, so they take it, and hand back a bishop or a rook for a knight.

The habit is the opposite. Leave the pinned piece exactly where it is, and
spend your moves bringing more attackers to it. It will still be there when
you are ready.

## What a pin is

Three things on one line: your piece at one end, an enemy piece in the middle,
and something the enemy cares about at the far end.

The piece in the middle is pinned. If it moves, the thing behind it is exposed.
That is all a pin is — a line, a blocker, and a reason not to unblock.

Only a queen, a rook or a bishop can pin, because only they attack along a
line. A knight can never pin anything.

## The absolute pin

When the piece at the far end is the **king**, the piece in the middle may not
move at all. This is not advice, it is the rules: you may never make a move
that leaves your own king in check, so every move of that piece is simply
illegal.

Two things follow, and both are worth more than they look.

1. **The piece is a free target.** It cannot run, so attack it again. A pawn is
   the ideal second attacker: it is the cheapest thing you own, and the pinned
   piece cannot take it without breaking a rule.
2. **The piece has stopped guarding.** A knight that may not move is not
   defending the squares it looks at. You can walk a rook onto one of them, and
   the knight can only watch.

The second one catches people out for years. Count the defenders of a square,
and then check whether any of them is pinned. A pinned defender does not count.

## The relative pin

When the piece at the far end is a queen or a rook rather than the king, the
piece in the middle **is** allowed to move. It just costs.

Treat it as pinned anyway. Nobody gives up a queen to save a knight, so in
practice the piece is as stuck as if the rules held it. The difference only
matters when your opponent is losing anyway — a player who is about to be
mated will happily break a relative pin, and an absolute one he cannot break at
all.

## Both sides of the board

Pins are not something that only happens to your opponent.

Before you move, ask two questions:

- **Is one of my pieces pinned?** You will reach for a knight, find the move
  illegal, and have to start thinking again with less time on the clock.
- **Is one of my defenders pinned?** This is the expensive one. A square you
  thought was guarded is not guarded at all, and the piece standing on it is
  hanging.

A pin that you do not see is worth more to your opponent than a pin you play.

## The skewer

A skewer is a pin the wrong way round. The valuable piece is in **front** and
the smaller one is behind.

Because the front piece is the valuable one, it moves — and when it moves, you
take whatever was hiding behind it. When the front piece is the king, it has no
say in the matter at all: it is in check, so it must move, and the piece behind
it comes off the board.

The test is the order, not the pieces:

- Small piece in front, big piece behind → **pin**. The front piece is stuck.
- Big piece in front, small piece behind → **skewer**. The front piece runs and
  the back one is lost.

So when you see two enemy pieces on one line, do not stop at "is that a pin?"
Ask which of the two is in front. If it is the king or the queen, you are not
looking for a way to freeze it — you are looking for a way to check it.

## Keeping the pin

Here is the mistake, written out in full so you recognise it in your own games.

Your rook on d1 is looking up the file at a knight on d7 with the queen behind
it on d8. You play Rxd7. The queen takes back. You have given five points for
three and the position is untangled.

Here is the same position played properly. You leave the knight alone and bring
a knight to e5, where it attacks d7 as well. Now the knight is attacked twice
and defended once, and it still may not run. Next move you win it for nothing.

One quiet move was the difference between losing two points and winning three.

The rule is short: **do not cash a pin in. Build on it.** Take the pinned piece
only when you have more attackers on it than your opponent has defenders — and
when that day comes, it will still be sitting there, because it never could
move in the first place.
