# Unit 2.5 — Discovered attacks

Appendix A gives this unit no habit of its own. What it gives you instead is a
way of looking: whenever two of your pieces are on one line, ask what the front
one is hiding.

## The idea in one sentence

**One piece moves out of the way and a different piece behind it does the
damage.**

That is all a discovered attack is. It feels strange the first few times,
because the piece you move is not the piece that attacks. You are not looking
for a good square for the knight. You are looking for a way to get the knight
out of your own rook's way.

## Finding one on the board

Look for a line — a file, a rank or a diagonal — with three things on it in this
order:

1. one of your long-range pieces (rook, bishop or queen),
2. one of your own pieces standing in its way,
3. something of the opponent's worth taking.

If you have all three, you have a discovered attack. The middle piece is the
door. Open it.

The same three things are worth looking for in the other direction, too. If
your opponent has a rook behind one of their own knights and your queen is at
the end of that file, the knight is not really stuck where it is. It can go
anywhere, and the moment it does, your queen is attacked.

## The mistake everyone makes first

The middle piece can usually move to six or eight squares, and **every one of
them opens the line.** That is what makes a discovery strong, and it is also
what makes it easy to waste.

If the knight just steps aside, the opponent simply moves the attacked piece and
nothing has happened. You have used a move; they have used a move; the position
is the same.

So the knight's move is free, and free moves should buy something. Send it to a
square where it takes a piece, or attacks a second one, or comes with check.
Then the opponent has two problems and one move.

## Discovered check

When the piece behind is aimed at the king, moving the front piece gives
**check** — and check changes everything.

A check has to be answered. Not "should be", has to: every legal move you make
while your king is in check must stop the check. So if your front piece has just
captured a queen on the far side of the board, none of the pieces guarding that
queen is allowed to take it back. They are busy, and they are busy for exactly
one move, which is all you needed.

This is why a discovered check wins material that looks perfectly well
defended. Count the defenders by all means, but then ask a second question:
**are any of them allowed to move?**

Two things to watch:

- **The king is not busy.** A king can answer a check by capturing, so a front
  piece that lands next to the enemy king can still be taken. Landing on f7
  beside a king on e8 is not the same as landing on g6.
- **The check can be blocked.** If the opponent can drop a piece on to the line
  between the checking piece and the king, the check is over and the recapture
  comes next move. Prefer the discovery that takes something big enough that it
  does not matter.

## Batteries

A **battery** is two of your pieces on one line, one behind the other, both
aimed at the same thing. Two rooks on a file. A queen with a rook behind her. A
bishop with the queen behind it on a diagonal.

It is the plainest tactic in chess and it decides a great many games. Something
guarded once cannot be taken by one attacker; it can be taken by two. Rxd7,
Nxd7, Rxd7 — you gave a rook and took a rook and a knight.

Three things follow from that:

- **Count before you capture.** How many of your pieces hit the square, how many
  of theirs defend it. If the numbers are equal, the capture only trades.
- **The front piece does not have to be safe.** It has to be *backed up*. A queen
  walking on to a square where a rook can take her is fine if your own rook takes
  the rook back — and better than fine if the recapture is mate.
- **A battery takes a move to build.** That move is worth spending. Bringing a
  second rook to the file is not a slow move; it is the move that makes the first
  rook mean something.

A discovery and a battery are two halves of the same habit. In a battery, the
two pieces on a line work together. In a discovery, the front one gets out of
the way. Either way, the first step is noticing that the line is there.
