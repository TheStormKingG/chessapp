# Unit 4.8 — Reading the position and making a plan

## The habit

**Name the pair, then play on your half of it.**

Section 3 taught you to look at a position and say which of the four elements is
out of balance. That was a diagnosis, and a diagnosis is not a plan. Unit 4.6
then gave you six permanent features to look for and told you to name the square
you would attack them from. This unit is the join between the two: how you get
from *what is out of balance here* to *and therefore I am going to do this*.

Five questions, in this order, and none of them takes long:

1. What is the pair that does not match?
2. Which side of the board does that pair point at?
3. Who is making the moves that have to be answered?
4. What is he trying to do over his next three or four moves?
5. Which swap would help me, and which would help him?

Everything in this unit is one of those five questions asked on a real board.

## What this unit is not

It is worth being precise about the three units next door, because the questions
look similar and are not.

- **Unit 3.6** asked *which element decides this position*. It stopped at the
  naming. Here the naming is the first line, not the last: the element is a pair,
  your thing against his thing, and the plan is inside the pair.
- **Unit 4.6** asked *where is the weakness and what square do I attack it from*.
  Those six features are permanent and static. This unit is about what you do
  when there is no obvious weakness to point at — which is most positions.
- **Unit 3.11** asked *what can he do to me next move*: every check, every
  capture. Lesson 4.8.4 asks a different question and it is worth seeing exactly
  how different. Every board in that lesson has been chosen so that Black has no
  check anywhere and no capture anywhere. Run the Section 3 scan on them and it
  returns the empty set. The plans are still there.

## The imbalances

An imbalance is always a pair. Not "White has a good knight" but "White's knight
against Black's bishop". Not "Black has a majority" but "Black's three pawns on
the kingside against White's two". Once you have both halves written down, the
plan is usually the shorter sentence: *play where my half is the better half*.

The pairs worth looking for, in rough order of how often they decide things:

- **Minor piece against minor piece.** Give activity a number by counting
  squares. A bishop with one move is not worth three, whatever the chart says.
  In the first lesson's board the bishop has exactly one square, its own pawns
  hold both its diagonals, and every single white piece — including the king —
  has more room than that.
- **Majority against majority.** Three pawns against two on one wing, and two
  against three on the other. Neither side is better. Both sides have been told
  where to play, and they have been told opposite things.
- **Rook activity.** One rook on an open file with somewhere to land against one
  rook with nowhere to go.

The knight's half of the first pair also shows you the shape of nearly every
piece plan in this section: find the square the enemy pawns can never attack
again, then walk the piece there. A square needs a pawn on one of the two
adjacent files, one rank further back, to be attacked. If both of those pawns
have already gone past, the square is yours for the rest of the game, because
pawns are the only men on the board that cannot take a decision back.

## Which side of the board to play on

Two counts, both of which take about five seconds.

**Count the men.** Take the three files of one wing and count everything on them,
pawns and pieces and the king. Do it for both sides. A one-man difference is
enough to choose by; a three-man difference is an instruction.

**Look at which way your pawns point.** A pawn chain points where its front pawn
looks. The pawn that has got furthest up the board is attacking two squares in
his half, and that direction is your side of the board. If the two counts agree,
stop thinking and start playing there.

Then, and this is the half that gets skipped: **prepare the push before you make
it.** A wing is opened by a pawn, and a pawn shoved forward without a friend
behind it is just a pawn you have given away. The same push, one move later, is
a swap he cannot decline. In the second lesson the immediate break loses a pawn
to a capture and the prepared one does not, and nothing about the position has
changed except the order.

## The initiative

The initiative has a definition you can count: **whose move has to be answered.**

The side that is answering is not making a plan. So the measure of a forcing move
is how much choice it takes away, and you can literally count that — legal moves
for him before your move, legal moves for him after it. In the third lesson's
first board Black has twenty-three legal moves, and White's only check cuts him
to two.

That number is half the sum. The other half is what the move cost, and this is
where the lesson lives: **forcing is not the same as good.** That same check, the
one that reduced twenty-three answers to two, loses a whole rook, because one of
the two answers is a capture and nothing defends the square. A move can be the
most forcing on the board and still be the worst.

The useful shape is the one that does both jobs at once. When one of your pieces
is attacked and nothing defends it, you have to move it; the move to choose is
the one that also takes something, or makes a threat of its own, so that the man
who was asking the questions is suddenly the one answering them.

## What does my opponent want

This is prophylaxis, and it is the question that separates this unit from
everything before it.

His checks and captures tell you what he can do **next move**. On a quiet board
the answer is nothing, and nothing is exactly when the question needs changing.
Ask instead: *what is he trying to do over his next three or four moves?*

The method is mechanical:

1. **Find the square his plan needs.** Nearly every plan at this level needs one
   square, and usually a pawn break: a pawn move that arrives next to one of your
   pawns. Look at his pawns and ask which of them has men lined up behind it.
2. **Count how many of your men cover that square.** Usually one.
3. **Make it two.** That is the whole of prophylaxis. It threatens nothing, wins
   nothing and attacks nothing, and his plan stops being available.

In the fourth lesson three black men are pointing at one pawn move; one white
pawn covers the square it wants; and one quiet pawn move makes it two. After
that his break is still legal, and it simply loses a pawn.

The tell that you are doing this right is that your move looks like it does
nothing. A move that takes away a plan never appears on a list of threats.

## Exchanging to a plan

Section 3 taught you to count the takers before calling something a swap. That
question is *is this sound*. This one is *of the sound swaps, which helps me*.

A swap is the cheapest way to remove one particular man from one particular
square. So:

1. **Name the square you want** — ideally one no enemy pawn can ever attack.
2. **Find the men covering it.** Often exactly one.
3. **Swap that one off.** Material stays level. The square changes hands.

In the fifth lesson a bishop is the only black man covering a square no black
pawn can ever reach again. Trading it off wins nothing at all — and afterwards a
rook walks in and nothing on the board can ask it a question.

Two corollaries worth keeping:

- **Whoever initiates the swap, the square still changes hands.** If his bishop
  is the guard, then his own capture also takes it off the guarding square. So
  the side that needs the guard should decline, and the side that wants the square
  should not mind who starts.
- **A piece you want to keep is not saved by running it somewhere loose.** If you
  do not want a piece swapped off, move it to a square he cannot reach, not to
  one where a pawn simply takes it.

## Using this both ways

- **What is my pair, and which half is mine?** If you cannot name both halves, you
  have a feeling rather than a plan.
- **Do my two wing counts agree?** Men on the three files, and the direction the
  front pawn looks. When they agree, that is your side of the board. When they
  disagree, you have not found the plan yet.
- **How many legal moves does he have after my move?** And separately: what did
  that move cost? Both numbers, every time, because the first one on its own has
  talked better players than you into losing a rook.
- **Which square does his plan need?** Ask it even when — especially when — he has
  no check and no capture. Then count how many of your men cover that square, and
  make it one more.
- **Which swap am I being offered, and who does it help?** Sound first, useful
  second. The swap that wins nothing and buys a permanent square is worth more
  than the swap that wins a pawn and opens a file for him.

The thread through all five lessons: a plan is a sentence with a square in it.
Judging a position tells you which pair is out of balance; the pair tells you
which wing; the wing tells you which square; and the square tells you which of
his men has to go. Everything else is counting.
