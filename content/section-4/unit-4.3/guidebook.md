# Unit 4.3 — In-between moves and defence

## The habit

**Active, not reactive. When something of yours is attacked, that is a question,
not an order.**

Everything in this unit is one idea seen from four sides. The idea is that the
move which suggests itself when you are under pressure — take it back, move it
away, defend it — is a move chosen by your opponent rather than by you. Three
of the four lessons here are about defending, and none of them is about
defending passively.

1. **The in-between move.** Before you take back, look for a check.
2. **Counter-attack.** Before you retreat, look for something bigger to hit.
3. **Blocking and selling.** When you must block, choose the piece. When a piece
   cannot be saved, choose what it takes with it.
4. **Threats two moves deep.** When his checks and captures come back empty, ask
   where his pieces want to stand.

## The in-between move

The German word is *Zwischenzug*, which just means "move in between", and the
whole technique is in the name. A capture happens. You are expected to take
back. Instead you play a forcing move first — almost always a check — he has to
answer it, and only then do you take back.

The capture does not go anywhere while you do this. That is the part that takes
getting used to: inserting a move costs you nothing, because the thing you were
going to do is still available afterwards. What it costs *him* is a free move.

Three things have to be true before an in-between move is worth playing:

- **It has to be forcing.** If he can ignore it, he will simply answer the
  original threat and you have lost a tempo. A check is forcing by definition,
  which is why nearly every in-between move in practice is a check.
- **The capture has to survive the insertion.** Look at the piece you are
  planning to take and ask whether his forced answer can save it, defend it or
  move it. Usually his answer is a king move and it cannot.
- **The immediate recapture has to be worse.** This is the one people skip. If
  taking back at once is just as good, you have not found an in-between move,
  you have found a transposition, and you have given him a free look at the
  position.

The commonest reason the immediate recapture is worse is that it hands him one
free move, and one free move is exactly what a back-rank mate needs. Two of this
unit's positions have that shape: the same capture is winning with check and
losing without it, and nothing about the capture changed. Only the order did.

The habit that catches these: **before your hand moves to take something back,
say your checks out loud in your head.** It is two seconds, and the list is
almost always one or two moves long.

## Counter-attack instead of retreat

A piece of yours is attacked. Your list of answers has four entries and people
only ever look at the first three:

1. Move it.
2. Defend it.
3. Take the attacker.
4. **Ignore it and make a bigger threat.**

The fourth is the one that wins games at this level, and there is a reason it
gets skipped. An attack on your piece is loud. It arrives with a little jolt of
alarm, and alarm makes the board shrink to the square the piece is standing on.
The cure is mechanical: when you notice something of yours is attacked, do not
start looking for squares. Start by asking **what can I hit that is worth more
than this?**

"Worth more" is the whole calculation, and it is arithmetic rather than
judgement:

- A mate threat outranks any piece. If you can threaten mate, a hanging knight
  is irrelevant — he does not have time to take it.
- A threat against a bigger piece outranks a threat against a smaller one.
- Two threats outrank one, even when each is small, because he can only answer
  one per move.

Then check the one thing that can go wrong: **can he answer your threat and
take your piece in the same move?** A capture that also defends, a check that
also attacks. If he has one of those, your counter-attack is not a
counter-attack, it is a gift.

There is a defensive version of the same arithmetic that is worth saying out
loud. When he takes the piece you offered, the position does not become "level
minus a piece". It becomes whatever your threat makes it. In both of this
unit's counter-attack positions he can take the piece you leave hanging, and in
both he is mated for it in two moves. Those lines are the point of the
exercise, not a footnote: you are not being brave, you are counting.

## Blocking: choose the piece, not the square

When you are in check, there are exactly three answers — move the king, take
the checker, block the line — and blocking is the one that needs a decision.
The others are usually forced or obvious. Blocking usually offers you a choice
of two or three pieces, and choosing wrongly loses on the spot.

Start from the fact that makes the choice necessary: **a blocker stands on the
line the checking piece is on, so the checking piece can always take it.** There
is no such thing as a safe block. The question is never "is it safe there", it
is "what happens after he takes it".

So run these three, in order:

1. **What defends the square?** If nothing does, you are giving the piece away.
   In this unit's clearest case, three moves all block the same check: one is
   mate next move, one loses a queen for a rook, and one is defended by a queen
   standing on a diagonal four squares away. Only the third is a move.
2. **Which is the cheapest piece that is defended there?** If two of your pieces
   are both defended on the blocking square, block with the smaller one. Trading
   a knight for a rook is a good afternoon; trading a queen for a rook is not.
3. **Is one of your blockers already in trouble?** This is the one worth
   remembering, because it looks like a coincidence and it happens constantly.
   If a piece of yours is being attacked by something *else*, and that piece can
   block the check, blocking with it solves both problems in one move. One
   position here has exactly that: a rook on the third rank is attacking your
   queen, the queen can block the check on the first rank, and blocking with her
   steps off the rook's rank at the same time. The other block leaves the queen
   to be taken.

## Selling a piece that cannot be saved

A piece that is attacked, undefended, and has no square that is not covered is
not a piece you own any more. It is one move you have left to spend, and the
loss is already in the position whatever you do next.

That changes the question completely. You are not looking for the square that
saves it, because there isn't one. You are looking at what it can reach, and
picking the most expensive thing on the list.

The part that goes wrong is the retreat. A retreat feels like the responsible
move, and it is available, and it is often to a square nothing is attacking —
which makes it feel verified. It is still the worst move on the board, because
it is the one line in which the piece goes and takes nothing with it. In one of
this unit's positions a knight has three squares: two are retreats and both are
covered by pawns, and the third is a rook. In another, the knight has four
squares and a single black queen covers every one of them, so there is nothing
to look for except the biggest capture.

The check that tells you which situation you are in is short:

- **List the squares the piece can move to.** All of them, including the
  captures.
- **Cross off the ones the opponent covers.** Remember that pawns cover squares
  diagonally even when those squares are empty, which is the commonest way this
  count goes wrong.
- **If nothing is left, list what each capture takes.** Pick the biggest one.
- **If something is left, you are not in this situation at all.** Go back and
  treat it as a normal attacked piece.

One more thing about being worse. Two of this unit's defensive positions are
objectively bad for you, and in both of them the right move still matters by
several hundred centipawns. Being worse is not the same as being lost, and the
difference between the best defence and the second best is often exactly the
difference between the two. A player who stops calculating when the evaluation
turns against him throws away the half of the game where defence is a skill.

## His threats, two moves deep

In Section 3 you learned a scan: his checks, his captures, his threats, run on
the board your move would leave behind. That scan is still right and it is still
the first thing to do. This lesson is about what to do when it comes back empty.

In every position in lesson four, Black has no check available and no capture
available. Not one. The scan you learned returns an empty list, and in all four
of them he is winning material or mating inside two moves. The threat is not a
move he can make now. It is a **square** one of his pieces wants, and the move
that gets it there is quiet.

So the scan grows one line:

1. Their checks.
2. Their captures.
3. Their threats.
4. **Where does each of their pieces want to stand?**

The fourth question sounds unbounded and it is not, because you only ask it
about your own weaknesses. Work backwards from what is loose:

- **Find your undefended pieces.** Every one of them. This is a ten-second
  count and it is the whole input to the next step.
- **For each one, ask which of his pieces could attack it in one move.** Not
  "does anything attack it" — that is the old scan, and the answer is already no.
  Ask which squares would attack it, and whether he can reach one.
- **Find your king's escape squares, or the fact that it has none.** A rook
  reaching your first rank is mate when the king is behind three unmoved pawns,
  so a rook one move from an open file is a mate threat even though it is
  currently doing nothing.

The answers come in three shapes and it is worth knowing them by name, because
recognising the shape is faster than calculating:

- **A bishop stepping on to a diagonal.** It joins a long line and something of
  yours is at the end of it. A bishop that looks passive is one move from being
  the most dangerous piece on the board.
- **A rook stepping on to a file.** Nearly always the file that runs into your
  first rank. Count which files are open and which of your own pieces and pawns
  are blocking them.
- **A knight stepping on to a fork.** Knights are the hardest of the three,
  because the square that forks is not on a line and does not look related to
  anything. If he has a knight within two squares of your position, spend the
  extra few seconds.

Your answers come in three shapes too:

- **Defend the target** rather than trying to stop him reaching the square. When
  a bishop can attack your rook from either side of it, you cannot cover both
  squares, and you do not need to — put a second rook behind the first and the
  capture becomes a trade.
- **Take the square away.** If the danger is one particular file or one
  particular square, occupy it yourself.
- **Move the target, and move it somewhere that does work.** A rook stepping off
  a fork and on to an attack makes him the one answering questions. This is the
  counter-attack lesson arriving again from the other direction, which is why
  the two sit in the same unit.

The habit sentence, if you only keep one: **when his forcing moves come back
empty, that is not an all-clear, it is a signal to change the question.**

## Putting it together

The four ideas are one process and it runs in this order, because each step is
cheaper than the one after it:

> Something happened. Is a capture pending — do I have a check to put in first?
> Is something of mine attacked — what can I hit that is worth more? Is it lost —
> what does it take with it? Am I in check — which of my pieces can afford to
> stand in the way? And if none of that applies: his checks and captures come
> back empty, so where do his pieces want to stand?

You will not run all five on every move. Run the first four whenever the
position has just changed in a way that cannot be changed back — a capture, a
check, a piece arriving on a new square. Run the fifth in the quiet positions,
which is exactly where people stop looking, and which is where the games between
1200 and 1600 are actually decided.

The thread through all of it: **the move that suggests itself under pressure was
suggested by your opponent.** Take the extra four seconds and choose your own.
