# Unit 4.9 — Rook endings

## The habit

**Ask what the rook is for.**

Every position in this unit turns on one question, and it is never "what can I
take". A rook in an ending is a wall, a shelter, a shadow behind a pawn, or a
nuisance behind a king. Those four jobs cover almost everything, and the
material count comes a distant fifth. A rook that is guarding a pawn is doing
the one job that is worth nothing.

The second half of the habit is counting. How many files his king has left. How
many checks he has after your move. How many squares your rook sees on the rank.
These are small numbers and you can get them exactly right at the board, which
is more than can be said for most middlegame judgements.

## The Lucena bridge

A pawn on the seventh rank with your own king in front of it is not a won game
yet, and the reason is simple: the king has to step aside to let the pawn
through, and the moment it does, the enemy rook starts checking. It never has to
stop. Nothing about your extra pawn prevents a rook from giving check for ever.

So you build a shelter before you move the king at all.

- **Rook to the fourth rank.** Not because the fourth rank is special in itself,
  but because from there the rook can reach a square on every file. Whichever
  file the checks come up, the block is available.
- **Then walk the king out.**
- **When the check comes, block it with the rook.** That block is the bridge.

The test of whether it has worked is a count you can do: after the block, how
many checks does he have left? In the lesson's positions the answer is one, and
that one is a capture of your rook — which hands over a rook to stop a pawn that
then queens.

Two details that cost people the win:

- The block has to be on a square his rook does not already cover. Right rank,
  wrong square, is still a lost rook.
- A pawn move instead of the rook move is the standard way to throw this away.
  The pawn is already as far as it needs to be; it is the king that is stuck.

## The Philidor defence

Now the same ending from the other side, where you are the one a pawn down. Rook
and pawn against rook is usually held, and it is held by a method rather than by
resourcefulness.

**While the pawn is on the fifth, put your rook on your own third rank** — the
sixth rank of the board, if you are Black. From there it covers the whole rank,
and the attacking king can never step in front of its own pawn. Count the
attacking king's moves once your rook is placed: in the lesson's position it has
four, and not one of them is forward. A king that cannot advance cannot escort
anything.

**When the pawn advances to the sixth, it takes that rank away from you.** That
is the signal for the second half of the defence: drop the rook right back and
check the king from long range. It has no shelter, because its own pawn is in
the way of the only square that would give it one.

The one trap here is the square you drop back to. If the enemy rook already
covers the first rank, going to the first rank loses your rook outright, and
losing the rook loses the game immediately. One rank higher checks from just as
far away and cannot be taken. Look along the rank before you land on it.

It is worth knowing what the engine will and will not tell you about this. A
defence that holds evaluates to 0.00, not to a plus score — so when you check
your own analysis, the drawing move is the one that scores level while everything
else is a large minus. In the lesson's main position the correct move scores 0.00
and the next best is nearly five pawns worse.

## The rook on the seventh

Unit 3.7 taught you to recognise this: get a rook to the seventh rank, and the
enemy pawns still sitting on their starting squares cannot run away from it,
because a pawn cannot move backwards. All of that still holds.

What changes in an ending is that **the other rook wants the same rank**, and
only one of you can have it. That turns the seventh into a question of one move,
and the move that settles it is a check.

The pattern to learn is this one. His king is on the seventh rank and so is his
rook. You arrive on the same rank with a check. He must answer the check — that
is the only thing a king in check may do — and while he answers it, your rook
takes his. In the lesson's position he has five legal replies and every one of
them loses the rook.

Two things follow:

- **Arriving second is not arriving.** If his rook already controls the rank,
  your rook lands there and is captured. The lesson has a position where Black's
  only move onto the seventh loses a rook for nothing.
- **The rook on the seventh is also a cage.** With his king on the eighth and
  your rook on the seventh, count his squares: often one or two, and both on the
  back row. That is worth more than a pawn and you should not trade it for one.

## Rooks behind passed pawns

Unit 3.7 taught you to spot a pawn that has got through. This is where the rook
goes, and the rule is short: **behind it.** Yours or his, behind it.

The reason is a count that runs in opposite directions.

- **Behind the pawn**, the rook attacks it. When the pawn advances, the rook
  still attacks it, and the rook now sees more of the file than it did before.
  The pawn cannot shake it off by running, because running brings it closer.
- **In front of the pawn**, the rook blocks it and nothing else. Every step the
  pawn takes removes one of the squares between them, so the rook's share of the
  file shrinks while the pawn's job gets easier.

The practical form of the rule, for the defender, is that a rook behind the
enemy passed pawn is doing real work while a rook in front of it is merely
parked. For the attacker it is the same rule from the other end: your own rook
belongs behind your own passed pawn, pushing it.

And a caution that the lesson makes into a question twice: **behind the pawn is
the right square only if it is available.** If his rook already sits on that
file above the pawn, going there loses yours.

## Cutting the king off

A rook in an ending is worth most as a wall. Put it on the file or the rank
between the enemy king and the thing the king is trying to reach, and then count
the files he has left. One file is a cage: his king can still move, and it has
stopped mattering.

The instinct this has to replace is checking. **A check is a free move for his
king.** He is obliged to answer it, but he chooses how, and what he usually
chooses is the square nearest the thing he wants. Every position in this lesson
has a check available and in every one of them the check is worse than the wall,
often by enough to turn a win into a draw.

Two practical points:

- The wall goes **beside** his king, not on top of it. A rook on his file gives
  check; a rook on the next file over gives him nothing.
- The wall must stand where his king cannot reach it. In one of the lesson's
  positions the rook is already touching the enemy king and has to move along
  its own file to a safe square — right file, wrong square, and it is simply
  taken.

## The active rook

This is the principle the rest of the unit keeps using, stated on its own.

**In a rook ending, activity is worth about a pawn.** A rook that is checking,
invading, sitting on the seventh or lurking behind a passed pawn is doing
something every move. A rook that is guarding a pawn on your own second rank is
doing one thing, for ever, and it cannot be persuaded to do anything else while
the pawn needs guarding.

So when the choice is between defending a pawn and activating the rook, take the
rook. Being a pawn down with the better rook is a position you hold; being level
with the worse rook is a position you lose slowly and without ever making an
identifiable mistake.

The test at the board is not "what is my rook attacking" but "what is my rook
going to be doing in three moves' time". A guard is doing the same thing in
three moves. An invader is not.

## Using this both ways

The four jobs, as a checklist, whichever side of the ending you are on:

- **Is there a passed pawn?** Get behind it. If the square behind it is
  occupied by his rook, find out whether you can contest the file at all before
  you commit.
- **Is his king trying to get somewhere?** Cut it off rather than check it, and
  count the files it has left afterwards.
- **Is the seventh rank available?** Take it, with a check if his king is
  standing on it, and do not trade it for a pawn.
- **Is my rook guarding something?** Then it is doing the worst of the four
  jobs. Look for a way to give the pawn up and get the rook out.

And the arithmetic that goes with each of them: checks remaining, files
remaining, squares on the rank, squares between rook and pawn. Rook endings
reward the player who counts, and in almost every case the count is small enough
to finish before you move.
