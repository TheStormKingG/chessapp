# Unit 4.2 — Interference, clearance and desperado

## The habit

**Before you look for a move, look for a line.**

Everything in this unit is about roads. A rook does not guard g7 because it is a
rook; it guards g7 because there is an unbroken row of empty squares between a7
and g7. A queen does not mate on g7 because she is a queen; she mates there
because nothing of yours is parked on the diagonal in between.

So the three motifs here are three answers to the same question — *whose road
is in the way, and what can I do about it?*

1. **Interference.** The road is theirs. Stand on it.
2. **Clearance.** The road is yours and one of your own men is on it. Move him.
3. **Desperado.** The piece has no road left at all. Sell it.

The first two are about lines. The third is about accounting.

## Interference

A defender defends *through* squares. List them, and you can attack them.

The trouble is that a single guard cannot usually be blocked, and it is worth
understanding why, because it stops you wasting moves. If a bishop on a3 guards
f8 through b4, c5, d6 and e7, then whatever you put on one of those squares is
the first thing on the bishop's road — so the bishop attacks it, takes it, and
lands on the road it was already using. The guard is not broken. It has just
moved closer.

Interference works when that recapture is not available, and by far the commonest
reason is that **there are two guards and one square**.

Take the position from the first lesson. The rook on a7 guards g7 along the
seventh rank: b7, c7, d7, e7, f7. The bishop on a3 guards f8 along its diagonal:
b4, c5, d6, e7. Write both lists out and exactly one name appears twice. Put a
knight on e7 and both roads are cut at the same moment. Black can restore either
one of them, but only by putting a piece on e7 — which is the other one's road.

- **Rxe7** saves the seventh rank and blocks the bishop. Rf8 is mate.
- **Bxe7** saves the diagonal and blocks the rook. Qg7 is mate.

The knight is not being sacrificed for a tempo or for a file. It is being
sacrificed for a square, and the square was chosen by arithmetic.

**How to find it over the board.** When two of your mating ideas each have
exactly one answer, do not go looking for a third idea. Write down the two
defensive roads and look for a square on both. If there is one, that is the move.
If there is not, there is no interference in the position and you should be
looking at something else.

## Line clearance

The mirror image: your own piece is the thing in the way.

This is much easier to miss than an enemy blocker, because you do not think of
your own men as obstacles. The tell is a piece that looks well placed — a queen
on the long diagonal, a rook doubled behind another rook — pointing at something
it cannot actually reach.

Two things matter.

**First, the clearing move usually does nothing on the square it lands on.** In
lesson 2 the pawn goes from e5 to e6, where it attacks nothing and defends
nothing. It is not a move *to* e6. It is a move *off* e5, and e6 happens to be
the only way of doing it.

**Second, and this is where the points go: a clearing move is a quiet move
unless you make it loud.** The moment you step aside, your opponent has a free
turn, and a free turn is usually enough to make an escape square and end the
whole attack. So look for the version that clears the line *with* something:

- with **check** — Rd4 to d8 is check, so Rxd8 is forced and the diagonal opens
  with Black's move already spent;
- with a **capture** the opponent has to answer;
- with a **threat** bigger than the one they were about to make.

If the only way to clear the line is a quiet move, count Black's defensive
resources honestly before you commit. Very often the answer is that the idea does
not work yet, and the right move is the one that takes the escape square away
first.

## Square clearance

Same idea, smaller target. Sometimes you do not need a whole line — you need one
square, and your own piece is standing on it.

The clearest case is a pawn on the seventh with one of your own pieces on the
promotion square. The pawn is not blockaded by the opponent; it is blockaded by
you. Every move of that piece unblocks it, so the choice between them is decided
entirely by what else the move does.

In lesson 2's example, Bb7 and Bc6 and Bd5 all clear a8. Two of them lose on the
spot to Rb1 mate. The third is check. That is the whole difference, and it lives
at the other end of the board from the pawn — which is the real lesson here.
**When you are choosing between moves that all achieve the same thing, the
tiebreak is never on the square you are looking at.**

## Desperado

Now stop thinking about lines and start thinking about arithmetic.

Some pieces are not in danger; they are already lost. A bishop on a7 has exactly
two squares, b8 and b6, because a7 is on the edge of the board. If both of those
are guarded, there is no move that saves it, and there is no move that will
appear next turn either.

The mistake is to keep looking for the rescue. The correct question is different:

> **It is coming off the board. What can it take with it?**

And once you ask it that way, the comparison becomes easy, because the loss is
identical in every line. Bxb8 and Bxb6 both end with a dead bishop. Three points
leave the board either way, so those three points cancel and you are left
comparing a rook against a pawn. That is not a close decision, and it is not
really a sacrifice at all — you are spending something you no longer own.

**Two things to watch for.**

- **Check the "trapped" claim properly.** A piece is only a desperado if *every*
  square it can reach loses it. Count the squares, one at a time, and ask who is
  looking at each. A piece with one safe retreat is not a desperado; it is a
  piece you should retreat.
- **Take with the piece that is dying, not the piece that is fine.** This is the
  version that comes up in real games. If two of your pieces are both attacked
  and you can only save one, the one that is doomed should be busy capturing
  while you save the other. Recaptures come in a fixed order, and the side to
  move gets to decide what that order is.

## Using all three the other way

When it is your position under pressure, the same three questions invert neatly:

- **Am I defending two things with two pieces whose lines cross?** That crossing
  square is where the sacrifice comes. Give one of the two jobs to somebody
  else while you still have a move.
- **Am I about to give my opponent a free turn?** If a piece of yours has to move
  and you have the choice, prefer the square that comes with a threat, even a
  small one.
- **Is one of my pieces already lost?** Then it is not a piece, it is a coupon.
  Spend it before your opponent gets to choose the moment.

A motif is only a name for a thing you have already seen once. The point of this
unit is the habit underneath all three: read the lines on the board before you
read the pieces on it.
