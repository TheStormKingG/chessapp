# Unit 4.7 — Bishops, knights and the isolated pawn

## The habit

**Judge the square before you judge the piece.**

Everything in this unit is one question asked four times. A bishop is not worth
three points; a bishop on an open diagonal is worth more than that and a bishop
behind its own pawn chain is worth less. A knight is not worth three points; a
knight on a square no pawn can attack is worth more and a knight on the rim is
worth less. A pawn is not a weakness because somebody called it isolated. It is
a weakness because of the square in front of it.

So when you are asked which side stands better, do not count. Name the squares.

## The bishop pair

One bishop touches thirty-two squares for the whole game. The other thirty-two
belong to whoever still has the bishop that covers them. Two bishops between
them touch all sixty-four, which is why the pair is worth something and either
bishop on its own is worth what the book says.

Two consequences worth holding on to.

**A knight always lands on the opposite colour to the square it left.** So a
knight on a light square can only go to dark squares, and in that moment only
one of the two bishops matters. List the knight's squares and you will find that
one bishop covers two or three of them every time. That is not a tactic; it is
arithmetic, and it happens on every move of the game.

**The pair is one advantage held by two pieces.** Trade either bishop and all of
it is gone at once — which is what makes it different from every other advantage
you own. The side with the pair avoids minor-piece trades and opens the
position; the side without it hunts for exactly one trade and then stops
worrying.

How much is it worth? About half a pawn, and it is worth being honest about that
number because it decides what to do with it. Half a pawn is not something you
cash in. It is something you hold while you look for a diagonal that lines up on
two pieces at once, and when one does, you take the exchange and give the pair
up without a second thought.

## Knight against bishop

The rule of thumb is that bishops prefer open positions with pawns on both
wings, and knights prefer blocked positions. That is true, and it is also not
what you should be checking.

What you should be checking is **mobility, counted**. In lesson 4.7.2 the
bishop on g7 sits on the longest diagonal on the board and has three legal
moves, because its own pawn on e5 is in the way and its own rook is on f8. A
knight with a permanent home beats a bishop with three squares, whatever the
general rule says about open positions.

### Outposts

An **outpost** is a square in the enemy half that no enemy pawn can ever attack.
Not "is not attacked right now" — can never be attacked. There are only ever two
pawns that could attack a given square: the ones on the files either side of it.
If both of those have already advanced past it, or are gone, the square is yours
for the rest of the game.

That test is worth running as a sentence, because it is easy to get wrong by
eye:

> Which two pawns could attack this square, and where are they?

Two mistakes to avoid.

**A defended square is not an outpost.** If a pawn can capture on the square, it
does not matter how many pieces are defending it. Three defenders lose a knight
for a pawn just as efficiently as none. Defence answers a different question.

**An outpost is a launch pad, not a parking space.** The knight sits there
because nothing can move it, but it is not doing its job by sitting. From d5 a
knight reaches c7 and e7 and b6 and f6, and one of those will eventually hit two
things at once. That is when the outpost pays, and it is the only time it does.

## The isolated queen's pawn

An isolated queen's pawn — an isolani — is a d-pawn with no friendly pawn on the
c-file or the e-file. It is neither good nor bad. It is a **trade**, and both
halves of it are real:

- **What it buys.** Space, and files. Give up your c- and e-pawns and the c-file
  and the e-file are open or half-open for your rooks, and your pieces get d4,
  e5 and c5 to stand on. This is why the isolani appears in openings people play
  on purpose.
- **What it costs.** A pawn that no pawn can ever defend, so pieces have to do
  it; and the square directly in front of it, which no pawn of yours can ever
  cover, for exactly the same reason.

The weakness and the hole arrive together and they are the same fact read twice.

So the plans write themselves, and they run on opposite clocks:

- **If it is your isolani, attack, and attack now.** Pieces are what pays for
  the pawn, so every pair that comes off the board makes your position worse.
- **If you are facing one, trade a pair of pieces and blockade.** In an endgame
  the pawn is just a pawn that cannot be defended.

### The blockade

Put a piece on the square in front of it. Three things follow.

The pawn cannot move, so it stops being a source of activity and becomes a
target. Your piece cannot be driven off, because the only pawns that could ever
attack that square are the two that were traded to make the pawn isolated in the
first place. And your piece is not blocked by anything: it sees the whole board
past a pawn that cannot advance.

**Blockade with the cheapest piece that does the job, and that is usually a
knight.** A blockader has to be able to stand still while being attacked, and a
queen cannot: one knight jump and she has to move or be lost. A knight on the
blockade square also keeps its own moves, because its moves are jumps.

### The isolani's one moment

The pawn has exactly one good move in its life, and it is forward. Pushed at the
right moment it stops being weak, opens the position for the side who has been
attacking all game, and very often hits two pieces on the way. A pawn arriving
on d5 attacks c6 and e6, and knights and rooks standing on those squares cannot
capture it back.

Which is the real reason to blockade: a piece on d5 means the push never
happens.

## Majorities, and making a passed pawn

Unit 3.7 taught you to **recognise** a passed pawn — its own file and the two
beside it, all three clear — and unit 3.9 taught you what to do with one:
push it, or stand in front of his. This lesson is the step before both of those.
You usually do not start with a passer. You start with a **majority**, and you
have to make one.

A majority is more pawns than he has on one wing. Three against two, four
against three. It is not a passed pawn and it does not become one by being
pushed at random.

**Count files, not pawns.** A passed pawn needs a file of its own, so a majority
is only worth something if it covers more files than his does. Four pawns on
three files against three pawns on three files produces nothing at all, which is
what doubled pawns really cost you: not the pawn, the file.

### Push the candidate

In a healthy majority one pawn has nothing opposite it. That pawn is the
**candidate**, and it goes first, with the others behind it as escort.

Push one of the others first and he meets it on the same file, the file shuts,
and the majority sits there for the rest of the game. This is the single most
common way a winning endgame is thrown away, and the fix is one sentence: look
up each file in turn and push the one with nothing on it.

### The outside majority

When both sides have a majority — which is the usual case, one per wing — the
one further from the kings is worth more. Its passer drags the enemy king to the
edge of the board to stop it, and while he is over there he is not defending
anything else. Your king walks in on the other side.

This is why an outside majority is an advantage in a position that is otherwise
dead level, and the count that decides it is king distance, taken as the larger
of the file gap and the rank gap. A king crosses diagonally as fast as it goes
sideways.

### The breakthrough

Sometimes there is no candidate: three pawns against three, every file
occupied. Then you buy one.

**Find the square that two of his pawns can capture on, and give a pawn there.**
With pawns on a5, b5 and c5 against a7, b7 and c7, that square is b6, and only
b6. Whichever pawn takes, it has left its own file empty, and the pawn behind
that file walks in: b6 axb6 c6 bxc6 a6, and the a-pawn queens. Start with a6 or
c6 instead and the b7 pawn simply takes it, because only one black pawn was ever
aimed at that square. The middle pawn first, always.

Two pawns for a queen is a good trade, but it is a trade and not a trick, so
count it before you play it:

- **How many of his pawns capture on the square?** One means you have lost a
  pawn. Two means it works.
- **How far away is his king?** The pawn that queens needs three moves, or four.
  Count his king's distance to the queening square as the larger of the two
  gaps. If he gets there, you have given two pawns for nothing.

## Using this both ways

- **Where are my holes?** Every pawn push gives away the two squares behind it
  on the files either side, permanently. That is not a reason never to push. It
  is a reason to name the square you are giving away before you push.
- **Is my bishop looking at its own pawns?** If so, it is not worth three, and
  the cure is usually a pawn move rather than a bishop move.
- **Which of my pawns can never be defended by a pawn?** Those are the ones that
  need pieces, and every piece tied to one is a piece not playing.
- **If we both queen, whose passer appears further from the kings?** Answer that
  before trading into a pawn ending, because it decides the whole thing.

The thread through all four lessons is the same. A piece is worth what its
squares are worth, and squares are decided by pawns — which are the only men on
the board that cannot take a decision back.
