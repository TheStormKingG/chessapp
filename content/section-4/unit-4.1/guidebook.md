# Unit 4.1 — Deflection and decoy

## The habit

**Name the guard, then count what it costs to move it.**

Unit 3.1 taught you to look one layer below the pieces, at the jobs they are
doing, and it gave you three ways to get rid of a guard: take it, overload it,
move it. The third one — deflection — got a first look there, and the examples
were cheap. A check the guard had to answer, a rook it had to take, and the bill
came to a rook.

At this level the bill is bigger and the guards defend properly. You will give
away a queen to move a bishop. That only works if you have counted two things
before you play, and beginners at 1200 count neither:

1. **Can the guard refuse?** List its legal answers. Not "it has to take" —
   *list them*. A guard with two answers needs both of them beaten, and the
   second one is usually the one that saves Black.
2. **Does every answer give the job up?** Sometimes a guard can capture your
   offer and still cover the square, or step into the way and cover it from
   there. Then the offer buys nothing.

Both questions are finite. Answer them on the board, not in your head.

## Deflection: pushing a guard away

A deflection makes the guard leave a square it was covering. The mechanism that
actually works is nearly always the same: **put something where the guard is the
only piece that can take it, and give check so it cannot decline.**

That is why a deflection tends to be expensive. You are not attacking the guard,
you are bribing it, and the bribe has to be big enough that the guard is the one
piece able to accept.

Two shapes are worth owning outright.

**The guard covers the square from a distance.** A bishop on c7 covering d8; a
knight on c6 covering the same square. Put a queen on b8 with check. The bishop
takes, or steps into the way, and either way it is no longer on c7. Then the
rook lands on d8.

**The guard is standing in your own piece's way.** This one is easier to miss,
because the guard is not guarding anything — it is *blocking*. A bishop sitting
on d5 in front of your rook on d1 is doing two jobs without meaning to: it stops
the rook, and it happens to be the only piece covering a8. Offer the rook on a8
with check, and the bishop that takes it has vacated the file. The rook it was
blocking now mates.

The second shape is the one to look for when a move you want is not available.
If the move is illegal because something is in the way, ask what that something
is attacking, and whether you can put a piece there.

## Decoy: pulling a guard onto a square

A decoy is the same act pointed the other way, and it is planned backwards.

Draw the position you want first. A knight on f7 gives check to a king on h8 and
attacks d8 at the same time — that is a picture, and it has nothing to do with
the position in front of you. Now look at the board: the knight can reach f7,
the queen is on d8, and the king is on g8 rather than h8. One square wrong.

So the whole combination is: **get the king to h8.** Put a rook on h8 with
check. The king takes it, because there is nothing else it can do, and now the
picture is on the board.

The difference from deflection matters when you are looking for the move:

- With a deflection you know the **square you want cleared**, and you are
  hunting for something the guard must take.
- With a decoy you know the **square you want occupied**, and you are hunting
  for something you can leave on it.

Three traps in decoys, all of which cost material if you miss them:

- **The king may not be the only piece that can recapture.** If another black
  piece can take on the square, it takes, the king stays where it was, and you
  have given a rook away. Check every capturer, not just the king.
- **A pawn can recapture on the forking square.** A knight forking from b6 wins
  nothing if a pawn on a7 takes it back next move. Look at the square the fork
  lands on as well as the square the king lands on.
- **The fork square may be defended by the king you are moving.** That is
  usually the whole reason the fork does not work yet, and it is also the reason
  the decoy does.

## The family, side by side

Five tools, and the guard picks which one you get. Work down the list:

| What you see | What to try |
| --- | --- |
| You can reach the guard and the price is right | **Take it** |
| The guard is doing two jobs | **Overload it** — attack one, it saves that one, the other falls |
| You cannot reach it, and it must take or must answer a check | **Deflect it** |
| The square you want the guard on is better for you than the square it is on | **Decoy it** |
| The guard cannot legally move | **It is pinned — attack it again, do not trade for it** |

Two things about the bottom row, because it is the one people get backwards.

A pinned piece is not a defender at all. It cannot step off the line to
recapture, so anything it is "defending" may be defended by nothing. And you do
not trade for a pinned piece, because it is not going anywhere: bring a second
attacker instead, and the cheapest second attacker is a pawn. A pawn attacking a
knight is a winning trade however many defenders the knight has.

When you cannot tell which tool a position wants, ask the two questions that
separate them:

- **What of mine actually attacks the guard?** If nothing does, taking and
  overloading are both off the board, and you are choosing between deflection
  and decoy.
- **Do I want the guard gone, or somewhere specific?** If you can name the
  square you want it on, it is a decoy. If you only want it off the square it is
  on, it is a deflection.

## Using this both ways

Turn all of it round when you are the one defending.

- **Which of my pieces is holding everything together, and can it be bought?**
  A piece that is the only capturer on some square is a piece that can be
  dragged there. Add a second defender to that square and the deflection stops
  working.
- **Is any of my pieces standing in front of one of my own?** Then it has a job
  it does not know about, and moving it may open a file at my own king.
- **Where is my king one square away from a fork?** Look at the knight's reach
  before you accept a sacrifice. If the only legal recapture puts your king on a
  square a knight already covers, the sacrifice is not a gift.
- **What is pinned?** Whatever it is, do not count it as a defender, and expect
  a pawn to come at it. The cure is usually to step the king off the line rather
  than to pile up defenders.

The unit is still about looking one layer below the pieces. What is new is the
price: at this level the right move usually looks like a blunder for exactly one
move, and the only thing that tells you otherwise is having counted the guard's
replies before you played.
