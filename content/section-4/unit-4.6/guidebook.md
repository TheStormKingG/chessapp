# Unit 4.6 — Stean's six

## The habit

**Name the weakness, then name the square you will attack it from.**

Section 3 taught you to *see* these things. An open file, a backward pawn, a hole
in front of it. You could point at them, and pointing was the whole exercise.

That is not enough any more. At this level your opponent can see them too, and
sees them at the same moment you do. What separates you from him is the second
half of the sentence: not *there is a weak pawn on c6*, but *there is a weak pawn
on c6, my rooks reach it down the c-file, it has one defender and I can bring
three attackers, and it cannot run because the square in front of it is covered*.

Six themes, and one shape underneath all of them. Each is a permanent feature of
the position — permanent because pawns cannot move backwards — and each is worth
nothing until you have counted what it takes to use it.

## The counting rule

Almost every lesson in this unit ends in the same arithmetic, so learn it once.

When you attack something that is defended, the captures happen in turn. You
take, he takes, you take, he takes. With **equal numbers** the defender makes the
last capture and comes out ahead — which is why two rooks against two defenders
wins nothing at all. With **one more attacker than he has defenders**, the last
capture is yours, and the thing is simply won.

That is the whole of it, with two footnotes that matter more than the rule:

- **A pawn defender is free.** A pawn guarding a pawn costs its owner nothing, so
  piling pieces onto a pawn that a pawn defends is wasted work however many
  attackers you gather. Either remove the pawn defender first, or pick a
  different target.
- **A piece behind a piece still counts.** Two rooks on one file are two
  attackers even though only the front one touches the square. So are a queen
  and a rook. Count the line, not the men that can move there this turn.

## Outposts and holes

A **hole** is a square in your opponent's camp that no pawn of his can ever
attack again, because both pawns that could have covered it — the ones on the
files either side — have already gone past it, and pawns do not come back. That
is what makes it worth playing for: you can spend two or three moves routing a
knight there and nobody can undo the work with one pawn push.

An **outpost** is a hole with your own pawn behind it holding the square.

The distinction is the whole lesson. A hole is a fact about pawns and says
nothing at all about pieces. Drop a knight into a hole that three of his pieces
are watching and you lose it, permanence and all. So the order is: find the hole,
count the takers, and only then work out the route.

Routing is the part that feels slow and is not. A knight on f1 cannot reach d5,
and of its three squares only e3 leads there — so the move is Ne3, and it is
worth a tempo because the square it ends on can never be taken away.

## Weak pawns

You already know the three faults: isolated, doubled, backward. They are one
fault wearing three coats — *no pawn of ours can defend it*.

Attacking one is a job with three steps, in this order:

1. **Can I reach it?** A weakness on a file you cannot get to is not a weakness,
   it is a fact. Two pawns can both be undefendable and only one of them worth a
   move.
2. **Can it run?** A pawn that advances out of trouble takes the weakness with
   it. Fix it first — often with a pawn of your own covering the square in front
   of it — and only then start gathering attackers. Bringing a knight up a move
   early lets the pawn push with tempo and you have gained nothing.
3. **Do I out-count it?** See the counting rule. Two against two holds.

The commonest mistake at this level is doing step three first, because it is the
one that feels like progress.

## Open and half-open files

Getting the rook to the file was Section 3. The question now is what it does
there, and the answer depends on which kind of file you have.

An **open file** carries no pawn of either colour. It is a road, and a road needs
somewhere to arrive. If every square on it in his half is covered — by a pawn, by
a knight, by two rooks guarding the back rank — then both your rooks are staring
at a wall and the file is worth precisely nothing. Before you fight for an open
file, name the square you intend to land on. If you cannot name one, play
somewhere else, or make one with a pawn break.

A **half-open file** carries only his pawns. That is easier, because the target
is already standing on it and your rook can do its work from your own half of the
board without ever entering his. Aim at the **base of the chain**: the pawn at
the bottom, which is the one no pawn behind it can defend. Take the base and the
pawn above it loses its defender too.

## Colour complexes and the good and bad bishop

A bishop touches half the squares on the board and never the other half, for the
whole game. So does every pawn standing in its way.

The measurement is blunt: **count your own pawns on your bishop's colour.** Six
of them and the bishop has one legal move and is not really a piece. None of them
and it is worth more than three. The cure is a pawn move or a trade, never a
bishop move — putting a bad bishop on a nicer square does not move the pawns.

Turn that round and it becomes an attacking plan. When his pawns have all left
one colour *and* the bishop of that colour is gone, every square of that colour
is yours permanently. Not a hole, a whole complex. His knight can visit those
squares; his remaining bishop never can, in any position it will ever reach. Look
for the one square in the complex that nothing defends at all, and put your
heaviest piece on it.

## Space

Space is the room behind your pawns, and it is two countable numbers rather than
a feeling:

- how many empty squares on his half do your pawns attack?
- how many legal moves does each side have?

Four against two on the first, thirty-four against twenty-five on the second,
and you have a space advantage whatever the material says. The symptom is always the
same: a rook with nowhere to go, a bishop with one square or none, pieces standing
on each other's toes.

One rule follows, and it reads both ways. **Cramped: trade. Roomy: keep them
on.** Space is squares per piece, and the cramped side cannot add squares, so he
subtracts pieces instead — every swap leaves the survivors somewhere to stand. A
space advantage that gets traded away was never cashed in.

And gaining space is free only while the pawn that gains it is safe. A push that
loses a pawn has not bought room, it has bought a draught.

## The minority attack

Two pawns marching at three, and giving up your own majority on that wing to do
it. It looks like the wrong end of the fight until you see what is being bought.

In the Carlsbad structure White has a2 and b2 against a7, b7 and c6, and no
c-pawn — so the c-file is half open for him already. The march is b4, then b5
hitting c6. Right now c6 is guarded by the pawn on b7, and that defence is free.
After bxc6 bxc6 the b-pawn is gone for good, no black pawn will ever reach b7 or
d7 again, and c6 is a pawn that only pieces can defend, standing on a file the
attacker has no pawn on.

White ends up with one pawn there against two, which is worse arithmetic, and
with a permanent target, which is worth more. **The point is the weakness, not
the pawn.**

Two practical notes:

- Do not take on c6 before the march. One rook against a pawn and a rook loses a
  rook. The march exists to strip the pawn defender, which is what makes the
  target attackable at all.
- Black's simplest answer is to meet b4 with b5 himself, occupying the break
  square with a pawn his own c6 defends. Then the break has to come with a4
  instead, and that is a slower job.

## The five questions

Ask these at any quiet moment, in this order.

- **Which squares can his pawns never attack again?** Those are the holes. Which
  of them can I reach, and hold with a pawn?
- **Which of his pawns can no pawn of his defend?** Of those, which can I
  actually reach, and can it run?
- **If I own a file, what square on it can I land on?** No answer means the file
  is not a plan yet.
- **How many of my pawns are on my bishop's colour?** And is there a colour he
  has abandoned entirely?
- **Who has more room, counted?** Then trade or keep pieces on accordingly.

Every one of these is a question about pawns, because pawns are the only men on
the board that cannot take a decision back. Everything permanent in a position is
their doing.
