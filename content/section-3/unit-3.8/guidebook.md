# Unit 3.8 — King safety and attacking the king

## The habit

**Do not weaken the pawns in front of your king without a reason.**

The king is not safe because he is in the corner. He is safe because three
pawns stand in front of him and nothing of the opponent's can get past them.
Every pawn you push is a door you cannot shut again, and the price is never
paid on the move you push it — it is paid four moves later, when a queen lands
on the square that pawn used to cover.

That is the defensive half. The attacking half is the same sentence read
backwards: you cannot get at a king through a wall of pawns, so your job is to
make a hole in it, and then to put two pieces on the line that runs through the
hole.

## The shelter, and the two ways it breaks

Three squares matter for a king on g1 or g8: f2, g2 and h2, or f7, g7 and h7.
A pawn on each of them is a wall. Move one and you get one of two holes.

- **The pawn steps forward.** ...g6 does not defend g7 any more. Nothing does
  except the king, and a queen that lands on g7 with something behind her is
  mate.
- **The pawn disappears.** Then the file it stood on is a road. A queen or a
  rook that reaches that file is next to the king with no pawn in between.

Both of those are the *same* defect seen twice: a square beside the king that
no pawn answers for. When you look at a king — yours or theirs — name those
three squares and ask which of them a pawn still covers.

## When not to castle

Castling is a move, not a rule, and it can be the losing move. The test is
short and you can run it before you touch the king: **count what attacks the
squares round the corner you are castling into, and count what defends them
once the king has arrived.**

The classic accident is h2 (or h7): a queen on h4 and a bishop on d6 both hit
it, the rook that used to sit on h1 has gone to f1, and the only defender left
is the king himself. Castling into that is mate on the spot.

Two practical rules follow:

- **Do not castle into an attack that is already built.** Break the line
  first — a piece on the diagonal, or a move that makes the queen retreat — and
  then castle.
- **Do not repair the shelter with a pawn move if you can help it.** g3 to stop
  a queen on h4 works, and it also hands the attacker the dark squares round
  your king for the rest of the game. A piece on the diagonal is usually the
  better answer.

The same arithmetic goes the other way: an opponent who has not castled yet may
be about to castle into your pieces, and the best move is often the one that
keeps your attackers where they are.

## Batteries

Two pieces on one line, one behind the other. The front one lands next to the
king and the back one is the reason the king may not take it. Everything about
using them is about **which piece goes in front.**

- **On a diagonal:** the queen in front, the bishop behind. The queen reaches
  the square beside the king, the bishop holds her there, and it is mate. Put
  them the other way round and only the bishop can land — which is safe, and
  still only worth a pawn.
- **On a file:** the rook in front, the queen behind. The rook is the piece you
  can afford to lose if something goes wrong, and the queen behind it covers
  the landing square.
- **The order is not a preference.** A piece cannot move through the piece in
  front of it, so whoever is in front is the only one who can land at all. If
  the wrong one is in front, the move you want does not exist.

Build the line before you need it. A queen and a bishop aimed at h7 from two
moves away are worth more than a queen on h7 that gets taken.

## Opening lines with pawn advances

Your pawns are the cheapest attackers you own, and they are the only pieces
that can knock a hole in a wall of other pawns and then *stay there*. Two things
a pawn on the sixth rank can do that no piece can:

1. **Capture, and be recaptured into a hole.** hxg6 followed by ...fxg6 has
   opened the h-file; hxg6 followed by ...hxg6 has opened the h-file the other
   way. Either recapture leaves the king with less cover than he had.
2. **Sit on a square and take it away for good.** A pawn on h6 covers g7. It
   cannot be captured by a king two squares away and it cannot be captured by
   pawns that only take diagonally forward. So the queen gets g7 for free, and
   a queen on g7 beside a king is mate.

The choice between **push** and **capture** decides a lot of these positions,
and the question is always the same: *what do I still own afterwards?* If the
capture trades away the pawn that was going to hold your queen's landing
square, push instead. If the push runs into a wall and the capture opens a
file your rook is already on, capture.

One caution. A pawn storm is a commitment: the pawns you send are the pawns
that were sheltering your own king. Storm when your king is somewhere else —
castled on the other side, or safe behind a closed centre — and be much more
careful when both kings are on the same wing.

## Recognising an attack coming

An attack is never one move. It is built, and the building is visible for
several moves before anything happens. Four things to look at, in order:

1. **Count the attackers.** How many enemy pieces touch one of the five squares
   around your king? Two is normal. Three is a warning. Four means something is
   about to land.
2. **Count the defenders.** Not the pieces near your king — the pieces that
   actually cover those five squares. A knight on f6 that covers h7 is a
   defender; a rook on a8 is not, however close it looks on the diagram.
3. **Look for the hook.** A pawn of yours that has stepped forward — h6, g6 —
   is something a pawn or a bishop can hit. It is how a closed position gets
   opened.
4. **Ask who can come back.** If your queen and rooks are on the other wing and
   it would take three moves to bring one home, you do not have three moves.

When the count comes out against you, the answers are, in order of how often
they work:

- **Trade the attacking piece**, usually the queen. Three pieces without their
  queen are not an attack.
- **Add a defender to the square**, not to the general area.
- **Block the line**, with a piece rather than a pawn if you can.
- **Counter-attack**, which is the right answer far less often than it feels.

And the one thing that is almost never right: grabbing a pawn on the other wing
because the attack has not arrived yet. It is arriving.

## Using it both ways

Every question in this unit works from either chair.

- *Which of the three squares in front of my king has no pawn covering it?*
- *Do I have two pieces on one line pointing at his king, and are they the
  right way round?*
- *Which pawn of mine can march at him without opening my own king?*
- *If I count attackers against defenders round my king, who is winning that
  count — and what would the answer be after his next move?*

The habit is the last one. Do the count before you make a move somewhere else
on the board, because the move somewhere else on the board is when the attack
arrives.
