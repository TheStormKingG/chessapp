# Unit 3.1 — Removing the defender and exploiting pins

## The habit

**Find the guard before you find the move.**

Most of the time the winning move is already on the board and something small is
in the way. A queen cannot land beside the king because one bishop covers the
square. A rook cannot reach the eighth rank because one knight watches it. The
move is not missing. The guard is.

So before you hunt for a combination, name the guard. Then ask the only question
that matters about it: what would it cost to get rid of it?

## Three ways to get rid of a guard

One lesson each, and you try them roughly in this order:

1. **Take it.** Pay whatever it costs, if what you are buying is worth more.
2. **Overload it.** Give it a second job it cannot do at the same time.
3. **Move it.** Check it, or offer it something it has to take.

The unit's fourth lesson is not about guards at all. It is about a piece that
cannot move in the first place: the pin, which is handled differently.

## Capture the guard

A piece is safe because something guards it. A square is closed because
something watches it. Take the guard and the door opens.

The price is the thing beginners get wrong in both directions. Giving a rook for
a bishop is terrible if you were only winning a pawn, and it is a bargain if you
were buying mate. So work out what you are buying first:

- If you are buying **material**, the guard has to cost less than what it guards.
- If you are buying **a square**, and the square is mate, the guard can cost
  anything you own.

When a square has two guards, take them in order, and remember that a piece
which recaptures is still a guard — it has simply moved to the square it was
defending. That is often where your second attacker takes it.

## Overloading

Some defenders are doing two jobs at once: holding a loose piece and watching a
square you want. A piece can only do one job at a time. Take one of the two
things it is guarding, and whichever one it saves, the other one falls.

The tell is a piece that appears twice when you list the defenders. Count the
jobs, not the pieces. One rook on the eighth rank defending a knight on the
fifth is two jobs, and either of them is enough to lose the game.

Overloading almost always looks like a bad trade for one move. You offer
something on the square where the defender is strong, and the answer comes on
the square it has just left.

## Winning a pinned piece

A pinned piece cannot run away. That is the whole point of it, and it changes
the arithmetic.

Normally you only take a piece when the exchange comes out in your favour. A
pinned piece is different: it is not going anywhere, so you never have to trade
for it on its terms. Bring a second attacker and take it next move instead.

The cheapest attacker is a pawn, and a pawn is the great leveller here. Three
defenders do not save a knight from a pawn: if the pawn takes and is recaptured,
you have given one point for three. Look for a pawn that can step forward and
attack the pinned piece without being taken.

Two things to hold on to:

- **A pin stops one piece moving. It does not stop anything else.** The pinned
  knight may be frozen, but the pawn beside it can still recapture, which is
  why taking the pinned piece at once is usually only a trade.
- **A pinned piece is a poor defender.** It cannot legally step off the line to
  recapture, so anything it is "defending" may not be defended at all.

## Deflection, the first look

Sometimes you cannot take the guard. It is too well defended, or it is the king.
Then make it move.

A check it has to answer, or an offer it has to take, and for one move it is
standing somewhere else. One move is all you need, because the square it was
guarding is now free and you have a second piece aimed at it.

The version that catches people out is the one where the guard is dragged onto
the very square it was watching — a rook steps into the square with check, the
guard captures it, and the second rook takes the guard where it now stands. The
guard was never removed from the board by force. It was invited.

## Using this both ways

The same three questions work when it is your position under attack:

- **Which of my pieces is holding everything together?** That one is the target.
  If it is doing two jobs, give one of them to somebody else now, while you have
  a free move.
- **Is anything of mine pinned?** Then it is not defending what you think it is,
  and the cure is usually to step the king off the line rather than to add
  another defender.
- **Can I be checked into moving a defender?** Look at the squares a check could
  come from, not just at the pieces attacking you.

A guard you have noticed is a guard you can replace. The unit is really about
looking one layer below the pieces — at the jobs they are doing.
