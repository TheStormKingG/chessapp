# Unit 3.3 — Trapped pieces and promotion

## The habit

**Count the squares before you count the value.**

A piece is not worth three points because it is a bishop. It is worth three points
because it can go somewhere. Take every square away from it and it is worth
nothing at all, whatever the chart says — and the same arithmetic runs the other
way at the far end of the board, where a pawn that nobody can stop is already a
queen.

Both halves of this unit are the same habit. List the squares. Cross off the ones
that are covered and the ones the piece's own men are standing on. Then look at
what is left.

## Three things this unit teaches

1. **Trapping.** A piece with no safe square only needs a move that attacks it.
2. **Under-promotion.** A pawn on the seventh is a piece you have not chosen yet.
3. **Advanced pawns.** A pawn past the middle of the board attacks, ties down and
   promotes, and it costs a whole piece to stop.

## Trapping a piece

Trapping is counting, not calculation. Take the piece you are eyeing and list
every square it can reach. There are usually fewer than you think — a knight on
the edge has four, a knight in the corner has two, and a bishop that has wandered
behind enemy pawns often has one.

Then cross squares off for two different reasons:

- **Its own men are standing there.** A knight on h5 with black pawns on f6 and g7
  has already lost half its squares to its own side.
- **You cover it.** A pawn on e3 covers f4. Pawns on f2 and h2 both cover g3.

If nothing is left, the piece is trapped. It is not lost yet — it is perfectly
comfortable sitting where it is. What finishes it is a move that **attacks** it and
forces it to choose a square it does not want.

Two rules follow, and they are the ones people get wrong:

- **Trapped is not the same as attacked.** A trapped piece that nobody attacks can
  sit on its square all game.
- **The attacking move has to come now.** A trapped piece is usually one tempo from
  being rescued: a pawn push that defends it, or a pawn push that opens a retreat
  square its own army was blocking. Every quiet move you play is a move Black
  spends on exactly that.

The cheapest attacker is a pawn, because a pawn attacking a piece is a threat the
piece cannot answer by trading. Look for the pawn push first.

### Trapping yourself

The same counting protects you, and the classic mistake is a free pawn on the edge
of the enemy camp. A bishop that takes on a7 has won a pawn and can still walk
home through b6 and c5 — until the b-pawn steps to b6 and closes the diagonal
behind it. Then a7 leads to two squares, and a knight or a rook covering both is
enough.

So before you take a pawn deep in the other side's position, ask the question one
move later than feels natural: **what squares will this piece have after the reply?**

## Promotion tactics and under-promotion

Nine times out of ten, promote to a queen. The tenth time is worth knowing,
because it is usually the difference between winning and drawing.

There are exactly two reasons to take something other than a queen.

### A knight, because it gives check and forks

A queen and a knight cover completely different squares, and only one of them can
attack a king and a second piece from the same square in the way a knight does. If
the enemy king and a piece are a knight's move apart from your promotion square,
count what the knight wins.

Then do the arithmetic properly, because it is the part that trips people up:

- Promoting to a queen gains you a queen: nine.
- Promoting to a knight gains you a knight and wins whatever it forks.

So a knight promotion that forks a **queen** is worth twelve and beats the queen
promotion comfortably. A knight promotion that forks a **rook** is worth eight,
and the queen promotion is better. Under-promotion is not clever by itself; it is
only right when the sum says so.

One more check before you play it: after the knight takes the piece, **can anything
take the knight?** If the enemy king was next to the piece it was forking, you have
traded a knight for a queen instead of winning one. That is still good, but a queen
promotion was better.

### A rook, because a queen would be too strong

This is the one that costs whole points. When the enemy king has one square left
and everything else of his is frozen, a queen takes that square too — and a king
with no legal move who is *not* in check is stalemate. A draw from a winning
position.

A rook covers the file and the rank and nothing else. The diagonal square stays
open, the king has a move, and you still have a completely won game.

The tell is always the same: **before you promote, count the enemy king's squares
and check whether anything else of his can move.** If the answer is "one square,
and nothing else moves", you are in stalemate country, and the piece that covers
fewer squares is the piece you want.

Two things worth holding on to:

- It is never a bishop. A bishop covers the diagonal a rook leaves open and cannot
  mate on its own.
- The king will usually attack your new rook on the next move. That is fine. Move
  it away; rook and king against a king that cannot move its pawns is over quickly.

## Advanced pawns as weapons

A pawn past the middle of the board stops behaving like a pawn:

- It **attacks two squares** that pieces cannot afford to stand on, and a piece
  cannot answer a pawn's attack by trading.
- It **costs a whole piece to stop**, so the defender is tied down.
- It is **one or two moves from being a queen**, which means every threat it makes
  has a second threat behind it.

So push it at something. A pawn that only goes forward is a pawn; a pawn that
arrives attacking two pieces is a fork you paid one point for.

Three patterns to look for:

- **The pawn fork.** Two enemy pieces on the same rank with one square between
  them. Push into the gap — and check first that a second pawn is holding the
  square you land on, so the push cannot simply be captured.
- **The push that is check.** If one of the two forked pieces is the king, the
  defender does not even get to choose which piece to save.
- **The promotion fork.** When the push lands on the seventh rank and forks two
  pieces on the eighth, taking either one makes a queen in the same move.

### The breakthrough

Three pawns against three, with the enemy king too far away, is a win and not a
draw, and the move is almost always the **middle** pawn.

Push it where both enemy pawns can take it. Whichever one takes, push the pawn on
the **far side of that capture** — the side the capture just abandoned. It runs
through on the third move.

The arithmetic is worth saying out loud, because giving two pawns away feels wrong:
you are not counting pawns, you are counting moves. Two pawns for one queen is a
bargain, and the only thing that can spoil it is the enemy king getting back in
time. Which is why the break has to be played **now** — every quiet move hands the
defending king one square of the journey home.

## Using this both ways

- **Which of my pieces has the fewest squares?** That is the one that gets trapped.
  A knight on the rim, a bishop that took a pawn, a piece behind its own pawns.
- **Am I about to take a free pawn?** Count the taker's squares *after* the reply,
  not before.
- **Do I have a pawn past the fifth rank?** It is not a pawn any more. Find
  something for it to attack.
- **Am I about to promote?** Count the enemy king's squares first. One square left
  and nothing else moving means a rook, not a queen.
