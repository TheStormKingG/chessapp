# Unit 3.6 — Judging a position: the four elements

## The habit

**Name the element, then name the side.**

Every unit before this one had a move to find. This one does not. Here the
question is the one you actually ask yourself at the board twenty times a game:
*am I doing well?* And the honest answer is almost never a feeling. It is a
short list you walk down.

Four things decide who stands better:

1. **Material** — who has more wood.
2. **King safety** — whose king can be got at.
3. **Piece activity** — whose pieces are doing something.
4. **Pawn structure** — whose pawns will still be healthy in an hour.

Walk the list in that order. Most positions are decided by **one** of the four,
and the other three are roughly level. When you can say which one it is, you have
judged the position. When the best you can manage is "White is a bit better on
balance", you have not judged it — you have guessed.

## Counting the elements

**Material** is the easy one, and it is first for that reason: it is the only
element you can count exactly. Queen nine, rook five, bishop and knight three,
pawn one. If one side is a whole piece up and nothing else is strange, the
judgement is over. Do not go looking for something cleverer.

**King safety** is about the pawns in front of the king and the lines pointing at
it. A king behind three of its own pawns is a fortress. The same king with those
three pawns gone is a target, even with the identical material on the board. Two
questions: are the pawns still there, and is a file or diagonal open towards him?

**Piece activity** is the one beginners skip, because it is the only element with
no number attached. So give it one: **count the squares a piece can go to.** A
knight with seven squares is a real knight. A knight with none is a spectator,
and the side that owns it is effectively playing a piece short.

**Pawn structure** is the slowest of the four and the one that does not go away.
Pawns cannot move backwards, so a pawn weakness you make in move ten is still
there in move fifty. Three things to look for: pawns **doubled** on a file, pawns
**isolated** with no friendly pawn beside them, and pawns **passed**, with
nothing left to stop them.

## What a bad piece is really worth

Here is the thing nobody tells you, and it is worth more than any rule on this
page.

In the second position of lesson 3.6.2 the black knight sits on a8 with its own
pawns on b6 and c7. It has **no legal move at all**, and with a rook and a knight
each still on the board that is worth nearly two pawns to White.

Now take both rooks off and change nothing else. The knight is still stuck on a8,
still with no move — and White's edge falls to under a pawn.

Why? Because a bad piece costs you in proportion to what else is on the board. If
you have almost nothing to play with, the piece with no squares is a small
annoyance. If there is a game going on and you are fighting it a piece short,
that is the whole game. **The fewer pieces left, the less a bad piece matters.**

Which is exactly why the next lesson is about trading.

## Good pieces and bad pieces

A piece is not good or bad because of what it is. It is good or bad because of
where it is and what is in its way.

The commonest bad piece in chess is a bishop with its own pawns sitting on its
colour. In lesson 3.6.2 the bishop on c8 has pawns on b7 and d7 — the only two
squares it can reach — so it has nowhere to go, and Black is effectively down a
bishop for as long as that lasts.

The commonest good piece is a knight on a square no enemy pawn can ever attack,
or a rook that has reached the seventh rank. A rook on the seventh does not rake
the whole rank, whatever it looks like — it stops at the first pawn it meets in
each direction. But those pawns cannot run, and the rook behind them decides what
happens next.

Two rules that follow:

- **A piece with no squares is not on the board.** Count before you argue.
- **Pawns make pieces bad.** Yours as often as theirs. Before you push a pawn,
  look at which of your own pieces it shuts in.

## When to trade and when to keep pieces on

A trade is two captures, not one. Before you call anything a trade, **count the
takers**: if they cannot take back, it is not a trade, it is a free piece — and
if three of their pieces can take back and only one of yours can, it is not a
trade either, it is a gift.

Once it really is a trade, four rules cover almost everything:

- **Ahead in material? Trade pieces.** Every pair that comes off makes your extra
  piece a bigger share of what is left. Trading rooks when you are a pawn up is
  how a pawn becomes a win.
- **Behind in material? Keep pieces on.** You need complications, and
  complications need pieces.
- **Never trade a good piece for a bad one.** This is the one that costs points.
  A knight sitting on a square nothing can attack is worth far more than the
  bishop that has been staring at its own pawns all game — and swapping them off
  is the opponent's dearest wish. In lesson 3.6.3 that swap costs more than two
  pawns' worth of advantage, and it looks completely natural.
- **Cramped? Trade.** If your pieces are treading on each other, every swap gives
  the rest more room.

The trap in all four is that a trade feels safe. It is a move that simplifies, and
simplifying feels like nothing can go wrong. But a trade is a permanent decision
about which pieces you have to play with for the rest of the game, and you cannot
take it back.

## Putting it together

Walk the list, in order, and stop at the first element that is clearly out of
balance:

1. Count the material. Different? That is your answer unless something screams.
2. Look at both kings. Pawns gone? Lines open? That is your answer.
3. Count squares for the pieces that look odd. Somebody a piece short in practice?
   That is your answer.
4. Look at the pawns. Doubled, isolated, passed?

And say it out loud as one sentence with a *because* in it: "Black is better
**because** his rook is on the second rank and White's is doing nothing." If you
cannot get the *because*, the position is probably level, and level is a perfectly
good answer.

## Using this both ways

The list is just as useful pointed at yourself:

- **Which of my pieces has the fewest squares?** That is the piece to improve, and
  improving it is often a better move than any attack you were considering.
- **Am I about to trade my best piece?** Look at what you would be getting for it
  before you look at whether it is "equal".
- **What did that pawn move cost me?** Every pawn push takes squares away from
  somebody. Sometimes it is your own bishop.
- **Which element am I winning?** Then steer towards it. Ahead in material, trade
  down. Better pieces, keep them on and open lines. Better pawns, head for the
  endgame.

Judging a position is not a talent. It is a list, walked slowly, in the same order
every time.
