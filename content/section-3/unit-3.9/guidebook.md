# Unit 3.9 — Endgames continued

## The habit

**Count before you move.**

Everything in this unit is arithmetic. How many squares between the kings. How
many moves the pawn needs, and how many the king needs. Which three squares win
the ending outright. Middlegames reward a good eye; these endings reward a good
count, and the count is nearly always small enough to do in your head.

The other half of the habit is knowing when to stop calculating. Several of
these endings are already decided — a rook pawn with the defending king in the
corner is drawn, and no amount of shuffling changes it. Recognising a settled
position saves you from losing it.

## Distant and diagonal opposition

You met the direct opposition in unit 2.7: two kings on the same file with one
square between them, and whoever is *not* to move has the better of it. The
whole idea generalises, and this is where most players stop too early.

The kings do not have to be two squares apart. They have to be on a shared line
— a file, a rank or a diagonal — with an **odd** number of squares between them.
One square between is the direct opposition. Three or five is the distant
opposition. A diagonal works exactly the same way.

The test is the same every time:

- Are the kings on a file, a rank or a diagonal together?
- Is the number of squares between them odd?
- Is it the *other* player's move?

Three yeses and the standoff is yours. The player who has to move first is the
player who has to give ground, and in a king-and-pawn ending giving ground is
usually the whole game.

When the answer is no, the move you want is the one that makes it yes. From a1
against a king on b8, that move is Kb2 — not because b2 is a good square in any
general sense, but because it makes the count five and hands over the move.

## King and pawn against king, complete

This ending is solved, and you can carry the solution in one sentence.

For a pawn on any file except the a- and h-files, take the three squares **two
ranks in front of it**. A pawn on d2 gives you c4, d4 and e4. Those are the
**key squares**. If your king stands on one of them, the pawn queens — it does
not matter whose move it is, and it does not matter where the defending king
stands. The only proviso is the obvious one: your pawn must not be hanging while
you do it.

That is the whole ending from the attacking side, and it explains the two rules
you were given earlier:

- **The king goes first.** The pawn cannot reach a key square; only the king
  can. Pushing the pawn before the king is in place is the standard way to throw
  a win away, and in several positions in this unit the two pawn pushes are
  among the losing moves.
- **The opposition matters because of the key squares.** Winning the standoff is
  how the king forces its way onto one of the three squares. It is a method, not
  an aim.

From the defending side the job is the mirror image: keep the enemy king off all
three, which in practice means getting in front of the pawn and staying there.
Note that being on the right file is not enough. A defending king may have three
squares available on the pawn's own file and only one of them will hold.

### The rook-pawn exception

An a-pawn or an h-pawn has no key squares worth the name, because the three
squares two ranks in front of it run off the edge of the board. If the defending
king reaches a8 in front of an a-pawn, or h8 in front of an h-pawn, the game is
drawn from any set-up whatsoever — attacking king on the sixth, seventh, it
makes no difference. The usual finish is stalemate: the defending king ends up
with no legal move and no check.

So with a rook pawn, the defender has exactly one job — reach the corner — and
the attacker has exactly one hope, which is to stop him getting there.

## Queen against a pawn on the seventh

A queen normally stops a pawn without effort. A pawn one step from queening,
with its own king beside it, is the exception, and it is worth knowing which
files are which.

The winning method against a **knight pawn or a centre pawn** is a loop:

1. Check the enemy king, or attack the queening square, so that the king is
   forced to step **in front of its own pawn**.
2. The pawn is now blocked by its own king, so you have a free move. Spend it
   walking your king one square closer.
3. Repeat. Eventually your king is close enough that the pawn falls or the king
   is mated.

Against a **rook pawn or a bishop pawn** the loop breaks, and it breaks for the
same reason both times: the defending king, driven into the corner, has no legal
move at all, so winning the pawn stalemates him. With a c-pawn on c2 and the
black king in the corner on a1, taking the pawn with the queen can leave Black
with nothing to move. Half a point, from a position that looks entirely won.

The exception to the exception is worth a look, because it is the one case where
a rook pawn loses on the spot: if the defending king is on a1 with its own pawn
on a2, the pawn is a wall of its own making, and a queen arriving on c1 is mate.
The corner only saves the defender when the defender can still breathe in it.

## Rook against a pawn

A rook beats a pawn comfortably when it gets **in front** of it — on the
queening square, or anywhere on the file ahead of it. From there the pawn cannot
advance at all and the rook simply waits for its king.

Behind the pawn is much worse than in front of it, because a pawn escorted by
its king shrugs off a rook that is merely chasing. The two things worth
remembering:

- **Cut the king off.** A rook standing on a rank or file that the enemy king
  cannot cross is often worth more than a rook that is attacking something.
- **Let it queen, and take the queen.** A rook on the same rank, file or
  diagonal as the new queen can win it with a check — the skewer from unit 2.3,
  turning up in an ending. If you cannot stop the pawn, look for the check that
  collects what it becomes.

## Passed pawns must be pushed and blockaded

A pawn is passed when nothing stands in front of it and no enemy pawn sits on
either neighbouring file. It cannot be stopped by pawns, which is why the only
answers to it are a king or a piece standing in its path.

Both halves of the title are practical advice:

- **Push it.** A tempo spent improving your king while a passed pawn waits is a
  tempo the defender uses to arrive. In this unit's positions, pushing and
  waiting are usually the difference between a win and a draw — and the margin
  is one move.
- **Blockade it.** Put something immovable directly in front of it. A king does
  this well; a knight does it best of all, because a knight on the blockading
  square still attacks things elsewhere. A piece that merely attacks the pawn
  from behind or beside is not a blockade, and the pawn will walk past it.

And the count that goes with it: **the square of the pawn.** From the pawn's
square, mark out the box whose side is the number of moves the pawn still needs.
If the defending king is inside that box — or can step into it — the pawn is
caught. If not, it queens. Remember that a pawn still on its starting rank may
jump two, so count it as though it had already moved one.

## Using this both ways

The same three questions work whichever side of the ending you are on:

- **What is the count?** Squares between the kings, moves for the pawn, moves
  for the king. Write the numbers down in your head before choosing.
- **Is this position already decided?** A rook pawn with the king in its corner,
  a defending king on a key square, a pawn outside the square of the enemy king
  — these are settled, and the right move is the one that keeps them settled.
- **Am I pushing the pawn because it helps, or because I cannot think of
  anything else?** In an ending with one pawn, a pointless pawn move is worse
  than a pointless king move, because a pawn never comes back.
