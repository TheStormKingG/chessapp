# Unit 3.12 — Story games

## The habit

**Play the game, not the puzzle.**

Every other unit hands you a position and tells you something is there. This one
hands you a game and tells you nothing. You are given a real position from a
real sequence of moves, you play the move you would play, and then you are told
what was actually played and why.

That is the whole difference, and it is the difference between knowing a motif
and finding one. A puzzle announces itself. A game does not.

## How a story game works

Each lesson walks through one complete game. The challenges are consecutive
positions from that game, in order, and your job at each one is to find the move
that was played.

Not every move gets a challenge. Most moves are obvious, or recaptures, or the
only legal answer to a check, and asking you for those would teach nothing. The
challenges are the places a reader would stop — and the prose between them
carries the moves you did not have to find ("Black recaptured, and now…"), which
is how Chernev does it in *Logical Chess*.

When the move is settled, right or revealed, a paragraph appears below the
board. That paragraph is the lesson. It is not the coach: the coach reacts to
what you just did, and the paragraph says what the move meant in the game,
which is true whichever way you answered.

## The four games

### 3.12.1 Morphy at the Opera

Paris, 1858: Morphy against the Duke of Brunswick and Count Isouard, who were
consulting over one board at the opera. Seventeen moves, out of copyright for a
century and a half, and the most taught game in chess.

It is here first because every idea in it has already been taught in Section 3.
**Removing the defender** (3.1.1) is the whole of moves 13 and 16: a rook and
then a queen given away to empty one file. The **pin** on f6 (3.1.3) is move 9.
The finish is the **Opera mate** by name (3.4.1). The game is not here because
it is famous; it is here because the learner can already read it.

The move worth stopping on is **14.Rd1** — quiet, nothing taken, nothing
threatened, the last piece simply arriving. Everything else in the game is a
capture or a check, which is exactly why that one is the hard question.

### 3.12.2 A 1000-level game, guess the move

*Premise: the classic bishop sacrifice on h7, and the discovered check that
finishes it — because three pieces were aimed at one square and one defender
had been traded off.*

A composed game, not a historical one. Both sides play sensibly out of a Colle
set-up; Black's error is not a blunder but a tempo — one more developing move
(12...Bb7) at the moment the kingside needed attention.

Two things it teaches that a puzzle cannot. First, **Bxh7+ here is not a
sacrifice at all**: the knight on g5 guards h7, so `Kxh7` is not a legal move.
Count the guards before you call something a sacrifice. Second, the finish is a
**discovered check** (3.2.3): the bishop steps off h7 and the check arrives from
the queen behind it, so the bishop may go anywhere it likes — and it goes
somewhere safe.

### 3.12.3 A second 1000-level game

*Premise: a queen developed to e7, in front of her own king, and what a rook on
the open file does to her.*

Composed. Black's fourth move, 4...Qe7, is played constantly at this level and
is not yet a mistake. It becomes one when the e-file opens, which Black does
himself with 8...d5.

The unit-3.1 lesson this rehearses is **winning a pinned piece** (3.1.3): a
pinned piece cannot run away, so you do not have to trade for it on its terms.
Here the pinned piece is the queen, and the arithmetic needs no preparation at
all — a rook takes her and a rook comes back.

The last challenge is the one to watch: after Black grabs a bishop, **12.Qe2+**
wins it straight back because the check comes first. Check first, then count.

### 3.12.4 A third 1000-level game

*Premise: Black empties f7 with a pawn break, and the game ends in the smothered
mate — a double check and a queen given away for nothing.*

Composed, and it exists to put **Philidor's legacy** in the learner's hands as
one unit rather than as four separate puzzles: knight checks on f7, king steps
to g8, **double check** on h6 (3.2.2) drives him back to h8, queen goes to g8,
rook is forced to take, knight mates.

It needs exactly one thing, and it is not White's skill: **f7 must be empty**.
Black empties it on move 8 with ...f5, which looks active and opens a file for a
rook — at the cost of the square and the diagonal that this mate is made of.

The second detail worth stopping on is 11...Ng6. It looks like a regrouping.
What it actually does is take the one black piece that could have captured on
g8 out of range, and without it the queen sacrifice is refutable.

## Reading a game the way this unit asks you to

Three questions, in order, at every position:

1. **What did the last move change?** A game is a sequence, not a set of
   positions. The move before yours either opened something, closed something,
   or left something.
2. **What is the most forcing thing I own?** Checks, captures, threats — in
   that order, and only then the quiet moves.
3. **What am I buying?** Morphy gave a rook and then a queen. He was not
   counting material; he was buying one file. If you can name what a move buys,
   the price stops mattering.

## What the checkpoint tests

Not the games. The bank does not ask you to remember what was played on move
thirteen of anything — a memory test would be a poor use of thirty questions,
and it would reward the learner who played the lessons yesterday over the one
who understood them.

It asks about the **motifs the games showed**, in positions like the games'
rather than from them: removing the guard of a mating square, the bishop
sacrifice that is not a sacrifice, the discovered check, the double check, the
pin down an open file, and the smothered mate as a pattern you can recognise
from the shape of the board.
