# Unit 3.10 — Building a repertoire skeleton

## The habit

**Develop to aggressive squares and castle early.**

A repertoire is not a book. It is a short list: one opening you play as White, one
answer to 1.e4, one answer to 1.d4. Three of them, played every game, until the
positions stop being new.

What you carry into them is the thing this unit is really about. It is not a move
order. It is a plan — which squares your pieces want, where the king is going, and
which pawn break you are heading for. A move order runs out the first time your
opponent plays something that is not in it. A plan does not.

## Why plans beat lines

At this level almost every game leaves theory by move six or seven, and it usually
leaves it because the other player did not know theory either. If what you have is a
list, that moment is a cliff. If what you have is a plan, it is just the next move.

Three questions, and your repertoire should answer all three before the game starts:

1. **Where do my pieces belong?** Not "what is move five" — which square each piece
   is heading for, and why.
2. **Where does my king go?** Almost always short, and almost always soon.
3. **Which pawn break am I building towards?** One break, named, so you know what
   all the quiet moves are for.

There is a fourth question this app deliberately does not answer for you: what other
players at your rating actually play in these positions. That needs move-frequency
data by rating band, and nothing in this app holds it. Your own reviewed games are
the substitute, and they are a better one than they sound.

## White: the Italian

1.e4, 2.Nf3, 3.Bc4.

The bishop on c4 is the point. Its diagonal ends on f7, and in the opening f7 is
defended by the black king and by nothing else. That is not a threat by itself — one
bishop never mated anybody — but it means every position in the opening has a
pressure point already built into it.

After that the plan fills itself in:

- **c3 and d3.** The pawn on c3 is there to back up d4. The pawn on d3 holds e4 and
  opens the bishop on c1.
- **Castle short.** Before the break, not after it.
- **Rook to e1**, behind the e-pawn, ready for the file that is about to open.
- **Knight from b1 to d2, then f1, then g3.** Its natural square, c3, is taken by
  your own pawn, so it takes the long way round. Note the order: the rook has to
  leave f1 before the knight can use it.
- **Then d4**, when the pieces are ready.

The one sequence worth knowing by heart is what happens when Black grabs the e-pawn
with a knight. The pawn on d3 takes back, and that is a knight for a pawn. It comes
up often enough to be worth the memory.

Two beginner raids, both of which lose material:

- **Bxf7+ before it does anything.** The king takes, and you have spent a bishop on
  a pawn. The bishop's job is to *sit* on c4.
- **…Bxf2+ the other way.** Same arithmetic, same answer: the king takes.

## Black against 1.e4: the Caro-Kann

1…c6, and then 2…d5.

The whole point of the first move is the second one. A pawn on c6 means the pawn on
d5 has a pawn behind it rather than a piece, so when White takes there, a pawn takes
back and the middle stays even. The price is that the knight on b8 cannot use c6 and
goes to d7 instead.

Then one rule, and it decides the whole opening:

> **Get the light-squared bishop out of c8 before any pawn goes to e6.**

The bishop's road out is c8–d7–e6–f5. A pawn on e6 sits in the middle of it. Play
…e6 first and the bishop has one square, d7, for a long time. Play …Bf5 first and it
is outside the pawn chain for the rest of the game.

After the bishop is out, the rest is ordinary: …e6, a knight to f6 or d7, …Be7,
castle.

Two things White does about that bishop, and neither is a disaster:

- **Chase it with a knight to g3.** It steps back to g6 and keeps the same diagonal.
- **Push the h-pawn at it.** Answer with …h6 first, so that when the pawn arrives on
  h5 the bishop has h7 to go to.

The line to know by heart is the one where a knight drops into d6 and it is mate —
the pawn that would take it is pinned against the king by a queen on e2. Look at the
lesson board once and it will not happen to you.

## Black against 1.d4: the Queen's Gambit Declined

1.d4 d5 2.c4 e6.

Same job, other pawn. The pawn on e6 holds d5, so cxd5 is answered by exd5 and the
middle stays a pawn against a pawn. That is all "declining" means.

The pawn on c4 is not free, which is the other half of the reason to decline. Take it
and White plays e3 and then Bxc4, and the pawn is back. Take it and then try to
*hold* it with …b5 and it is worse than that: a4 opens the file, the pawns come off,
and the diagonal to a8 is open with nothing standing on b7.

Black's plan after 2…e6:

- **…Nf6**, which guards d5 a second time.
- **…Be7**, which unpins the knight if White has played Bg5, and clears the way to
  castle.
- **Castle short.**
- **…h6**, asking the bishop on g5 what it wants.
- **Then the bishop on c8** — which is the bill for the whole opening.

That last one is the part to actually plan for. With a pawn on e6 and a pawn on b7,
the bishop on c8 has exactly one square, d7, and if a knight goes to d7 first it has
none at all. The two ordinary cures are …dxc4 followed by …b6 and …Bb7, or …Nbd7 and
…b6 in the other order. Pick one and know it is coming.

## Using all three

Notice how much the three openings have in common. Every one of them is:

- a pawn in the middle, backed by a second pawn rather than a piece;
- the pieces out to squares chosen in advance, not to squares that looked active;
- the king castled before the position opens;
- one named pawn break to work towards.

That is why three openings is enough. You are not learning three sets of moves. You
are learning one way of thinking about the opening, and practising it from three
starting positions until the middlegames start to look familiar.

When somebody plays a move you have never seen — and at this level somebody will,
most games, usually before move eight — the answer is not to try to remember. It is
to ask the three questions again. Where do my pieces belong now? Is my king safe? Is
my break still on? Then play the move that answers one of them.
