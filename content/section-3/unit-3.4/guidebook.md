# Unit 3.4 — Named mates, first wave

## The habit

**Name the shape before you count the moves.**

Everything in this unit is a mate you could have found by calculating. The point
of learning the names is that you stop calculating. A player who knows the
Arabian mate does not work out that the knight guards the rook and covers the
corner; he sees a rook and a knight near a king in the corner and the answer
arrives before the analysis does.

That is the whole value, and it is worth being honest about what it is not. A
name is a memory aid, not a proof. Every one of these shapes still has to be
checked on the board in front of you, because the pattern and the position are
not the same thing.

## What a named mate is actually made of

Pull any of these twelve apart and you find the same three jobs, shared out
differently:

1. **Somebody gives check.** Usually the piece that lands next to the king.
2. **Somebody guards the checker**, so the king may not simply take it. This is
   the job beginners forget, and it is the job that makes the names worth
   knowing — it is nearly always a *quiet* piece a long way from the action.
3. **Somebody fills the escape squares.** Very often this is not White at all.
   It is the defender's own rook, or his own pawn, standing on a square his king
   now needs.

When you cannot find the mate, it is almost always job 2 or job 3 that is
missing. Ask which one, rather than looking for a different check.

## Lesson one: four mates on the edge

**Anastasia's mate** is a knight and a rook. The knight sits on e7 with the
black king on h7. From e7 the knight covers g6 *and* g8 — two escape squares for
one piece — and the king's own pawn on g7 takes a third. All that is left is the
h-file, and a rook arriving there finishes it.

**The Arabian mate** is the same two pieces standing closer together. The king
is on h8, the rook comes to h7 right beside him, and a knight on f6 guards the
rook so it cannot be taken. That same knight covers g8, and the rook itself
covers g7. Rook and knight, and nothing else on the board is needed.

**Greco's mate** brings in a bishop on the b1–h7 diagonal. The queen lands on
h7 next to the king and the bishop, sighting all the way down the diagonal,
guards her there. The king cannot take, the queen covers g7 herself, and one
more square is shut by Black's own man.

**The Opera mate** is a rook on the back rank with a bishop behind it on a
diagonal running into the same square. The bishop does two jobs at once: it
guards the rook, and it covers the diagonal square the king would otherwise step
to. Black's own pawn takes the last one.

## Lesson two: the long diagonal

Two of these three are the same idea one file apart, and the file is the whole
difference.

**Morphy's mate** starts with the rook standing *in its own bishop's way* on the
long diagonal. Move the rook to the h-file and two pieces check at once — the
rook up the file, the bishop along the diagonal it has just cleared. A double
check can only be answered by a king move, which is exactly why it is mate when
the king's last square is occupied by his own rook.

**Pillsbury's mate** puts the rook on the g-file instead, checking up an open
file at a king on g8, while a bishop on the long diagonal covers h8 behind him.
Here the king's other squares are all his own men. It is worth noticing that no
sacrifice is involved; what makes it possible is simply that the g-pawn has
gone.

**Boden's mate** needs no rook at all. Two bishops on crossing diagonals — one
checking through the square in front of the king, one covering the two squares
the first bishop cannot reach — against a king who has castled queenside and is
sitting behind his own rook and pawn. Because bishops on opposite colours can
never cover the same square, the two diagonals between them cover everything.

The classic way in is a queen sacrifice that drags a pawn off the checking
diagonal: the queen gives herself up on a square the pawn must take, and the
bishop comes in through the hole. Do not learn the sacrifice as a trick. Learn
that the bishop needs the diagonal, and then look at what is standing on it.

## Lesson three: who is doing the blocking

These four are best sorted by whose pieces are in the way.

**Lolli's mate** is White's: a pawn on f6 guarding a queen on g7. One pawn, and
because it is a pawn it can guard the queen from a square nothing can profitably
attack.

**The épaulette mate** is Black's, entirely. His rooks sit either side of the
king like a pair of epaulettes, and a queen checking up the file covers the three
squares in front. Nothing of White's takes the sideways squares, because the
defender has already taken them himself.

**The dovetail mate** is also Black's, but diagonally: the two squares that touch
the king corner-to-corner are his own men, and a defended queen lands beside him.
The V-shape those three squares make is where the name comes from.

**The hook mate** is a chain — a pawn guarding a knight, the knight guarding a
rook, and the rook giving check next to the king. Three white men, each one
holding up the one in front. It looks fragile and it is not: try to break the
chain and you find that the pawn is the only piece in it that nothing can take
profitably.

## Lesson four: blind swine

Two rooks doubled on the seventh rank have an old name — **blind swine** —
because of how often they turn out to do nothing at all. They look like the end
of the game. Whether they actually mate comes down to one question:

**Is the rank between them and the king clear?**

With the pawns still on f7, g7 and h7, the answer is no, and no amount of
admiring the rooks changes it. The first pawn stops the rank dead, so the rooks
never even reach the squares beside the king. The checks that are available are
on the eighth rank, and a defended back rank answers them by capturing.

Once the rank is clear the same two rooks are lethal, and for a reason worth
saying out loud: **each rook guards the other**. That is what doubling buys. A
rook landing next to the king is normally just a gift; a rook landing next to the
king with its partner sighting along the rank behind it is mate.

When the king is one square further away, you drive him: check on the square
beside him, he steps into the corner, take the pawn with check, he steps back,
and the second rook comes up to the square the first one has just vacated. Three
moves, and every black reply is forced.

## Using this both ways

The same three jobs tell you when *you* are the one in trouble:

- **Which of my own pieces is standing on a square my king needs?** This is the
  single most common ingredient in this whole unit, and it is the one you can do
  something about before the attack arrives. A rook on f8 is a fine rook and a
  terrible neighbour.
- **What is guarding the square beside my king?** If the answer is "only my
  king", then anything that lands there is mate the moment it is defended. Look
  for the quiet bishop or knight a long way off, not for the checking piece.
- **Is a file or a diagonal about to open?** Morphy's mate and the blind swine
  are both the same story: nothing changed about the attacking pieces, only about
  the line they were standing on.

A named mate you can see coming is a named mate that does not happen.
