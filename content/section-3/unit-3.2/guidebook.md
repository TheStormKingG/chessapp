# Unit 3.2 — X-ray, double check and discovered check

## The habit

**Look at the whole line, not the first piece on it.**

Unit 3.1 was about the guard. This one is about the line the guard is standing
on. All three ideas here are the same observation from different angles: a rook,
a bishop or a queen does not care very much what is standing in front of it,
because pieces move.

So when you look at a long-range piece, do not stop at the first thing it meets.
Ask what is behind that thing, and ask what happens the moment it steps aside.

## X-ray

An x-ray is a line that works *through* a piece. It comes in two flavours and
they are worth naming separately, because you use them at opposite moments.

**X-ray defence.** Your piece looks at a square, and an enemy piece is standing
in the way. Right now you are not defending that square. But if the enemy piece
captures on it, the piece has moved out of the way and onto the square in the
same move, and now you are.

That is why a rook guarded by a bishop is often not guarded at all. Take the
rook, let the bishop recapture, and take the bishop where it lands. The guard
never escaped; it walked one square forward into your line.

The tell: **the defender is on the same line as the square it is defending.**
Look for a bishop defending along its own diagonal, or a rook defending along
its own file, with one of your long-range pieces pointing down that same line
from behind.

**X-ray attack.** The same geometry, the other way round. Your rook looks down a
file; an enemy knight is on the file and an enemy rook is behind it. The knight
is not pinned in the strict sense — nothing illegal happens if it moves — but
moving it loses the rook. So treat it as frozen:

- do not take it, because it is usually defended and you would be paying too
  much;
- attack it again instead, and cheapest first. A pawn is the best attacker there
  is, because nothing wants to trade with it.

Then Black has to choose: give you the piece in front, or give you the piece
behind. Either is fine. That is the whole point of setting up two targets on one
line.

## Double check

Against a single check you always have three answers: capture the checker, block
the line, or move the king. Against two checkers at once you have one.

- You cannot capture both.
- No square sits on two different lines at the same time, so no block works. And
  a knight check can never be blocked at all.
- So the king moves. Always. Whatever else is happening on the board.

This is why a double check is worth so much. The checking piece may stand
anywhere: beside the king, on a square a pawn attacks, in the middle of three
defenders. None of it matters for one move, because none of those pieces is
allowed to move.

How to spot one: you need a piece sitting on a line between a long-range piece of
yours and the enemy king. When that piece moves, the line opens. If it *also*
gives check from wherever it lands, that is the double check. For knights, the
square almost always matters — one destination checks and the others do not, so
work out which one before you play it.

And when you are on the receiving end, count the checkers before you look for a
clever answer. If there are two, the only question is which square the king goes
to.

## Discovered check with gain

A discovered check is the same mechanism with the second check left out. One
piece moves, another piece checks.

The important thing is which piece does what. **The checker is the one that stays
still.** The piece that moves is not doing anything to the king, which means it
is completely free for one move. So use that move properly:

- take something that is defended — the defender cannot recapture, because that
  would not answer the check;
- take something and then walk the piece back to safety next move, since your
  opponent has spent his move on the king;
- or simply put the piece on a square it could never survive on otherwise.

The mistake is to play the discovery as though the check were the point. It
usually is not. A discovered check onto an empty square just gives your opponent
a free tempo to sort his king out. Ask what the moving piece can grab on its way.

The second mistake is to forget the difference between a discovered check and a
double check. A single discovered check *can* be answered by a block, and often
is — a queen or a rook drops onto the line, keeps itself safe, and the whole idea
evaporates. Before you play it, check whether the opponent has a piece that can
reach the line between the checker and the king.

## Putting the three together

They form a ladder, and it is worth going up it in order when you are looking for
a combination:

1. **Is anything of mine already lined up with something of his?** That is the
   x-ray. It costs nothing to set up and it freezes pieces.
2. **Is one of my pieces sitting on a line to his king?** Then every move that
   piece makes comes with a check attached. That is a free move — spend it.
3. **Does that piece also check from where it lands?** Then it is a double check,
   and nothing on the board can answer it except the king.

## Using this both ways

The defensive versions are just as useful:

- **Is one of my pieces on a line to my own king?** Then it can be sent away with
  check and there is nothing I can do about it. Move the king off the line, or
  move that piece before he does.
- **Am I defending a piece along the line it stands on?** Then my defender may be
  walking into an x-ray when it recaptures. Defend from somewhere off the line.
- **If he checks me with two pieces, I do not look for a capture.** I look for a
  square, and I look for it fast, because that is the only answer there is.
